What I changed from the originalBuilt for the rest between sets

The original is a good reaction test for a desk. A gym in the middle of a session is a different place, so these are the changes.

  • The rest is the bossThe rest clock never leaves the screen. A round starts only if there is enough rest left for a normal one, and the game stops the second the rest does. Nobody plays one more round into their next set.
  • Tennis questionsThe original asks one thing, how fast. These drills ask what a player asks on court. When was it hit? Which side? Is it in? When does it reach me?
  • No guessing the startThe wait before each hit is random, so you can't time it. Tap before the hit, or so soon after it that it can only be a guess, and it's a foot fault. That ball is played again.
  • No red and greenThe cue is movement and a burst, never a colour change alone. In most drills it is the ball leaving the clay racket at the top, which stands for you. About 1 in 12 men of European descent see red and green poorly, and somewhat fewer in most other groups. The original's main mode is a red screen turning green. Birch 2012
  • The middle numberA round shows the median, not the average. One tap missed while someone talks to them doesn't wreck it.
  • Your phone, your numbersEvery phone adds its own delay to the touch and the screen, and it differs from phone to phone. So bests are kept per phone and nobody is ranked. There is no "average human is 250 ms" grade that a slow phone fails for no reason.
  • Seen, never heardSound comes after the tap, never with the hit. A beep at the hit would be a second, faster cue, and a phone on silent would lose. The cue is visual only, so the game is not playable without sight. The rest timer and every other part of the app are unaffected.
  • A ball that sits upIn Sweet Spot the ball bounces and then slows by a different amount each time. In one small study, expert table tennis players timed a slowing target better than novices did, and a steady one did not tell them apart.
  • One thumb, wet handsThe targets are the whole court or half of it. Nothing small to hit with chalk or sweat on the screen.
  • Nothing leaves the phoneNo server and no XP. Nothing new for the privacy notice.

Easy to hardThe four drills

Drill 1

First Step

Tap the moment Amir hits the ball.Simple reaction. Five balls. The score is the median ms of your taps.
Drill 2

Fore or Back

Tap the side the ball goes to.A choice. Six balls, each left or right at random. The score is the median ms of the right taps, and how many were right.
Drill 3

In or Out

Tap if it lands in. Leave it if it's out. On the line is in.Tap or leave. Six balls, two of them out. The score is the median ms of your taps. A good leave counts toward a best.
Drill 4

Sweet Spot

Tap as the ball reaches your racket.Timing. Five balls. The score is how many ms off, and whether you lean early or late.

A best counts only when every ball was right and there were no foot faults. That keeps a fast guesser from setting one.

Checked before buildingWhat the research says

According to PubMed. The coloured line is the one that shaped the game most.

  • Phone time before lifting didn't cost strength in one study. After 30 minutes of social media or a Stroop task, 25 trained adults lifted the same bench press max and jumped as high, even though they felt more mentally tired. Alix-Fages 2023
  • Long scrolling can cost decisions. In amateur boxers, 30 minutes of social media or video games just before a fight simulation made decisions worse. In young volleyball players, 30 minutes before each session for four weeks stopped their decision-making from improving. A review of 12 papers found that demanding tasks of about 20 to 30 minutes tired the mind, and short or passive ones often did not. Fortes 2021 (boxers) · Fortes 2021 (volleyball) · Bian 2025 reviewSo the rounds are about 15 to 20 seconds, capped by the rest. That is far shorter than anything tested.
  • Expert players picked up sport cues faster and more accurately than non-experts, across 42 studies. Mann 2007 meta-analysisSo every drill asks a question from court.
  • Tennis players aged 10 to 14 timed a moving target with less error than table tennis players of the same age, who had the quicker simple reactions. Ak & Koçak 2010So Sweet Spot is the drill closest to what a tennis player is good at.
  • Expert table tennis players timed a slowing target better than novices, but not a steady one (16 people, on a screen). Ripoll & Latiri 1997So the ball slows by a different amount after the bounce each time.

What we don't claim. A phone game won't teach anyone to read a serve. It's a game for the rest, and the app should never call it reaction training.
