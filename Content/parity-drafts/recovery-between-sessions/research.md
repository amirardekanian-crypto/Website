### Q1. "What is the best way to recover after tennis?"

**Planned answer:** before buying another tool, fix the foundations (energy, carbohydrate, fluids, sleep, load). Tools are for specific situations, not magic. Recent evidence does not show one physical tool consistently beating passive recovery for sport-specific performance.

**Evidence**
- **Tools vs passive recovery.** Jin 2026 network meta-analysis (16 RCTs, 273 competitive endurance athletes): no modality beat passive recovery for soreness, CK or sport-specific performance (11 trials); compression garments are the one moderate-certainty finding and it is a null (SMD 0.04, CI -0.37 to 0.45); a sham immersion ranked at or above every active tool. [READ]
- **In tennis.** Wiewelhove 2021, nine ranked men, five 2.5-hour matches in five days: a four-tool bundle changed nothing (match play, load, soreness, CK, jump). Poignard 2023, 13 men, three match-like days: cold immersion or cryotherapy stopped soreness rising but performance was unchanged. Duffield 2014, 8 men: a bundle gave more time in play next session, from an abstract with mostly non-significant P values. [READ]
- **What tools do reliably:** lower soreness (Hohenauer 2015, Dupuy 2018, Davis 2020). Cold immersion also gave a small power gain at 24 h after high-intensity exercise (Moore 2022, SMD 0.22) and no strength gain. [READ / AGENT]
- **Foundations.** Carbohydrate about 1 g/kg/h refills glycogen faster when the next session is under 8 h away (Craven 2021). You must drink more than you lost, with enough sodium (Shirreffs 1996). Sleep extension and naps were the sleep interventions that worked; hygiene alone did not improve performance (Cunha 2023). One night of 5 h or less cut junior accuracy (Vitale 2021). Load spikes go with injury (Gabbett 2016, Soligard 2016, Johansson 2021). [READ / REUSED]

**Certainty:** moderate that tools do not reliably beat passive recovery on performance (several analyses agree, but certainty is low, nobody can be blinded, and the trials are young men and few in tennis). Moderate that the foundations matter as processes (glycogen, fluids, sleep are measured). **Low that foundations beat tools in tennis: that comparison has never been run.**

**Footnote to the planned answer:** "foundations first" rests on physiology, consensus statements (ITF 2025, Walsh 2021) and what goes wrong when they are missing (Vitale; pros start matches dehydrated, Hornery 2007 in BJSM). Say "that is where the biggest known losses come from", not "proven better than tools". "Tools don't beat passive recovery" is right for sport-specific performance; do not stretch it to "tools do nothing", because soreness drops and cold immersion has a small next-day power effect.

**Do not say:** "recovery tools are useless", "nothing works".

### Q2. "Why do I need recovery? What happens if I don't?"

**Planned answer:** because repeated training and matches accumulate fatigue and affect the qualities you need to play tennis.

**Evidence so far**
- **It stacks across days.** Four 4-hour matches on four days (Gescheit 2015): sprint and jump fell from day 2, CK, soreness and fatigue rose daily, players moved less at the same effort rating. Three 2-hour matches in three days (Ojala 2013): leg force and rate of force development lower before matches 2 and 3 and still low 1-2 days later. Five 2.5-hour matches (Wiewelhove 2021): soreness, CK, CRP, IGF-1 and jump all changed over the days, with or without a recovery package. [READ]
- **Two matches in one day cost the next day.** Next-day sprint -3.3%, agility -4 to -5%, jump -5%, shoulder strength -9 to -11% in 12 young players (Gallo-Salazar 2017). [READ]
- **Technique and composure.** More unforced errors by day 4, lower second-serve winning, more emotional outbursts (Gescheit 2016). One night of 5 h or less cut junior accuracy 14-24% while sprinting held (Vitale 2021). Mental fatigue built across same-day padel matches (Díaz-García 2021). [READ]
- **Over weeks: slumps (other sports and self-report).** 29% of 376 young athletes aged about 15 (club to international, 19 sports, not tennis-specific) reported at least one spell of persistent fatigue and a performance drop lasting weeks to months; load did not predict it, being elite, female or in an individual sport did (Matos 2011). Overreaching and overtraining are defined by time: days to weeks and weeks to months (Kreher 2012; IOC 2016). No tennis or padel prevalence exists. [READ]
- **Injury (associations).** Back pain rose 1.17 times per workload spike in 198 competitive juniors followed for 52 weeks (Johansson 2021); shoulder complaints 1.26 times per spike (Johansson 2021 shoulder, AGENT); in pro men more cumulative load went with more time lost (HR 1.05 per 1,000 games, Kovalchik 2020); Davis Cup retirements were 2.6 times as common in the last two matches of a tie and 5.5 times in best-of-5 matches (Casals 2024; a format effect, not proof of fatigue); under 8 h sleep went with 1.7 times the injury odds in adolescents (Milewski 2014). No trial shows that extra rest prevents injury. [READ / REUSED / AGENT]
- **Illness (association; sleep and colds shown in non-athletes).** Adults sleeping under 5-6 h had about 4 times the odds of a cold after being exposed to a virus (Prather 2015: 164 healthy adults, wide intervals). The IOC consensus links load spikes, congested calendars, travel, poor sleep and stress to illness (Schwellnus 2016). [READ]
- **Under-eating in heavy blocks (shown in a small tennis camp).** Ten 14-year-old academy players in a 6-day camp ate about 1,430 kcal a day less than they used and slept 6.9 h with 28 awakenings a night (Fleming 2022). [READ]
- **Burnout and quitting in juniors (association).** Recovery is one input among motivation, perfectionism, parental pressure and sport structure; high burnout-risk rates of 55% in girls and 16% in boys aged 8-15 come from one cross-sectional survey (AGENT). [section 11]
- **Counter-evidence:** planned hard blocks followed by rest raised fitness in pros, and planned junior camps did not cause overreaching in 12-14-year-olds (AGENT). The line the evidence supports is "hard training is fine; hard training without enough recovery is the problem", which is my wording, not a quoted finding.
- **What the mind and the game do (section 10):** mental fatigue (a 30-min thinking task before hitting) lowered groundstroke targeting about 15% in 66 male tennis players and raised failed second serves in 10 more; pro padel players' mental fatigue built between two same-day matches. In played matches the cost is small and sits in margin shots, with tactics shifting too; whether it changes results is not shown. [10b, 10c]
- **The physical side (10a) and the one-page map (10d):** legs about 9% weaker after 3 hours (heat: about 22%), shoulder rotation about 5% weaker, soreness and effort rising together; the plain-language map is in 10d.
- **More on warning signs, how much rest is enough and juniors:** section 11.

**Certainty:** strong that fatigue builds over consecutive heavy match days (several small tennis studies agree; the doses are extreme). Moderate for performance effects. **Association only for injury, illness and burnout.** Slumps are shown in other sports and by self-report; there is no tennis prevalence.

**Careful:** real pro draws were kinder than the lab marathons (Maquirriain 2016, López-Samanes 2018, AGENT). The honest rule is "match length times number of days in a row".

**Do not say:** "skip recovery and you will get injured" (association, not cause).

### Q3. "What should I do the night before a tennis match?"

**Planned answer:** eat normally with carbohydrate available, hydrate, no new foods, prepare your equipment, reduce unnecessary stress, give yourself enough time in bed, do not chase a magic protocol. The night before is not when you build fitness; it is when you avoid undoing what you have built.

**Evidence, item by item against the planned answer** (full sources in section 13)
- **"Eat normally with carbohydrate available."** Supported, with a refinement: a normal carbohydrate-rich dinner, not a "load". ITF: about 6-10 g/kg across the day before when training is lighter; classic loading (10-12 g/kg for 36-48 h) only "if practical" when five-set matches are possible; a pre-match meal of 1-4 g/kg, 1-4 h before play. Loading does nothing for 60-90 min of moderate exercise and delays fatigue by about 20% only beyond 90 min (Hawley 1997); trained men reached maximal muscle stores within 24 h of resting on 10 g/kg (Bussau 2002). Glycogen use across a tennis match has never been measured. Teenagers: eat enough, never "load" (a review found not enough evidence for loading in child athletes; the ITF says junior intakes are often below target). [READ / AGENT]
- **A familiar dinner, finished about 3-4 hours before bed.** A high-glycaemic carbohydrate meal 4 h before bed shortened sleep onset to 9.0 min, against 17.5 min for a low-glycaemic meal and 14.6 min for the same meal 1 h before bed, in 12 men (Afaghi 2007). Lower fat and fibre is practice-based; there is no experimental evidence (Costa 2025 position statement, agent). [READ / AGENT]
- **"Hydrate."** The value is arriving hydrated, so drink steadily through the day. ITF morning check: body mass within 1% and urine specific gravity under 1.020; 5-7 mL/kg about 4 h before play. Pros arrived at specific gravity 1.022 (14 men, Hornery in BJSM) and 1.026 (9 men, López-Samanes). A big fluid load late in the evening costs sleep (ITF consensus, no trial). Do not oversell it: a 2.7% loss slowed 5-10 m sprints but not serve or groundstrokes (Magal 2003, 11 men) and the cognitive cost is small (Wittbrodt 2018). [READ / AGENT]
- **"No new foods."** Practice-based with indirect support: two weeks of eating carbohydrate while running cut gut symptoms about 60% and raised 1-hour distance in 25 runners (Costa 2017). That trained carbohydrate during exercise, not the pre-match dinner. [READ]
- **"Enough time in bed."** Right, because time in bed is the part you control. Poor sleep before competition is common and situational: 65.8% of 632 German athletes had it at some point and 62.3% in the past year, mostly from not falling asleep, and most said it did not affect performance (Erlacher 2011); about two thirds of 283 elite Australians (Juliff 2015). The lab harm used about 5 h (Vitale 2021, Reyner & Horne 2013); the typical competition-night loss is about 1 h, measured after play (Roberts 2019); one to three nights of partial restriction are "unclear" (Walsh 2021); and **no study measures match results after one restricted night.** The better-supported lever is the week before: a few earlier nights and steady bed and wake times (Mah 2011, Schwartz & Simon 2015; Lever 2020 for juniors at tournaments). Wind-down: five minutes writing a specific to-do list got 57 adults to sleep faster than listing finished tasks (Scullin 2018). [READ / REUSED / AGENT]
- **Caffeine early, no alcohol.** Caffeine cost about 45 min of sleep across 24 studies and should stop about 8.8 h before bed (Gardiner 2023, agent); 400 mg disturbed sleep even 6 h before (Drake 2013). Alcohol fragments the second half of the night and cuts REM (agent) and cut muscle protein synthesis 24-37% even with protein (Parr 2014). None for teens. [REUSED / AGENT]
- **"Reduce unnecessary stress."** Fine, but nerves are normal: anxiety was lower by the second match in juniors and pros showed no pre-match cortisol rise (Villafaina 2022, López-Samanes 2018, agent). Simple routines (slow breathing, imagery, a pre-match routine) cut anxiety in small studies (100 elite juniors, no pure control); nobody tested them the night before. [AGENT]
- **"Prepare your equipment."** Practice-based; nothing to test.
- **"The night before isn't when you build fitness."** True: nothing builds fitness overnight. Two refinements. The night does settle fuel, fluid and sleep debt, so "only avoid undoing what you built" undersells it. And **do not back the line with detraining data**: Mujika & Padilla Part I shows that within 4 weeks of stopping, highly trained athletes lose VO2max and blood volume quickly while strength losses are limited. It supports "a week or two without heavy lifting costs little *strength*", which is what the site's line says, not "costs little fitness". For "cut volume, keep intensity" use Bosquet 2007 and Mujika & Padilla Part II. [READ]

**What the planned list is missing** (agent): in a tournament week every night is both a night before and a recovery night (refuel 1.0-1.5 g/kg/h for 4 h when the gap is short; evening starts cut sleep by about 80 min, Roberts 2019); the morning plan (a pre-match meal 1-4 h out, 5-7 mL/kg fluid, caffeine only if pre-tested); the week before matters more than one night; hotels (the first night in a new bed is worse, then recovers; bring familiar snacks; earplugs and an eye mask); unknown start times (a flexible meal plan, ITF); and a medicines, illness and anti-doping check (practice-based).

**If sleep or appetite goes wrong anyway** (agent): it is common. In bed: slow breathing, an imagery task (shortened onset in 41 insomniacs), no clock-watching. A nap of 20-90 min between 13:00 and 16:00, finished 30 min before play (Lastella 2021). Caffeine only if pre-tested, 3-6 mg/kg about 60 min before (Guest 2021); 80 mg did not rescue serving (Reyner & Horne). No appetite: light, easy carbohydrate, liquid carbohydrate, top-ups until the start (ITF).

**Avoid:** new foods and heavy, fatty or fibrous meals; alcohol; "just in case" ibuprofen (no evidence, gut and kidney risk); sleeping pills; melatonin gummies (content ranged from -83% to +478% of the label in 31 products, agent); trusting tracker scores (poor wake detection, n = 34, agent); hard or new sessions.

**Padel:** amateur matches last about 57 min, so loading is irrelevant at that length (Marcos-Rivero 2025); morning-after-elimination data show worse sleep, mental toughness and reaction time (Díaz-García 2023); no night-before study exists.

**Certainty:** carbohydrate and fluid = consensus plus non-tennis physiology; sleep = small trials, surveys and consensus; no study links night-before food, fluid or sleep to a tennis or padel match result.

**Footnotes to the planned answer (the calibrated pushback):**
1. "Eat normally with carbohydrate available": say a normal carbohydrate-rich dinner, not a load.
2. "Hydrate": the point is arriving hydrated, which starts the day before, but it is not a performance switch.
3. "Enough time in bed": right, and protect the week, not only the night. One bad night is common and not a disaster, and do not promise "9 hours the night before or you lose".
4. "Not when you build fitness": true, but the night does settle fuel, fluid and sleep, and the detraining paper does not support the usual "a week off costs little fitness".

### Q4. "What supplements should I take for recovery?"

**Planned answer:** before I say which supplement is good for recovery, check whether you need one at all.

**Evidence**
- **The frameworks agree on the order: food first, test before you supplement.** ITF 2025: supplement use is an informed decision, with contamination and doping risk; iron and vitamin D follow a blood test. IOC 2018: diet is assessed first, and the supplements with good evidence (caffeine, creatine, buffers, nitrate) are performance aids, none a recovery aid (web summary of the IOC text). The AIS programme grades supplements A to D by evidence, safety and doping risk and is built on "food first" (Burke 2026). The Sports Dietitians Australia statement for adolescents says needs should come from core foods rather than supplements, and to check calcium, vitamin D and iron (Desbrow 2014). [READ]
- **For recovery, the things that work are food.** Carbohydrate and fluid with sodium (Craven 2021, Shirreffs 1996). Protein after one bout: no consistent gain in soreness or strength recovery; the gain is clearer after daily sessions or when under-eating (Pasiakos 2014, 27 studies). Protein powder is convenience, not magic. [READ]
- **Small, mixed or against:** creatine (fewer damage markers 48-90 h after one hard bout but more at 24 h with chronic use, soreness not significant, Doma 2022; no effect on tennis strokes in one trial; the ITF advises against it for juniors), tart cherry, omega-3, curcumin, chocolate milk; BCAA and magnesium (no benefit; the Cochrane review found no trial of magnesium for exercise cramps); vitamin C/E and anti-inflammatory pills (blunt adaptation); alcohol. See 5b and section 12. [READ / AGENT]
- **Where a pill has clear evidence: a diagnosed gap.** 56% of 2,313 athletes had low vitamin D, more in the UK and Middle East, in winter and spring and in indoor sports (Farrokhyar 2015); iron treatment raised iron status and VO2max in iron-deficient, non-anaemic endurance athletes (Burden 2015). Test first. [READ]
- **Contamination is real, with old numbers:** 14.8% of 634 products bought in 2000-01 held undeclared steroids (Geyer 2004). [READ]
- **Tennis and padel:** 80-100% of elite players use at least one supplement (ITF). The trials are performance trials in small groups, and the racquet-sport review of 21 RCTs supports only caffeine (3-6 mg/kg, 30-60 min before, adults; Vicente-Salar 2020). **No recovery-supplement trial exists in tennis or padel.** [READ]

**The "do you need one?" ladder** (full version in 12b): name the problem (sleep, energy, carbohydrate and protein after sessions, fluid and salt, load); can food do it?; is there a diagnosed gap (test, then iron, vitamin D, calcium, B12 for vegans)?; is the gap practical (travel, no kitchen, no appetite) so a batch-tested food-format product helps?; under 18: only those steps with a parent and a doctor or dietitian; adults only and not recovery: caffeine and creatine.

**Certainty:** high that most recovery supplements give small or mixed effects; moderate that the ladder is the right frame (it is consensus, not a trial).

**What the research does to the site's current wording** (decisions for Amir; nothing has been changed): keep "food first" and the under-18 line. "At any age: food, not pills and powders" is stricter than any framework: it could carry a footnote for adults, travel and diagnosed deficiency. Name the "don't bother" list, date the contamination numbers, and say honestly that caffeine under 18 is a coaching rule backed by sleep and a lack of safety data, not a toxicity line (details in 12f).

**Do not say:** a shopping list; "BCAAs, magnesium, vitamin C, cherry juice or collagen speed recovery".

### Q5. "Does massage actually help recovery?"

**Planned answer:** massage can make you feel better, but does it make you perform better?

**Evidence**
- **Feel:** massage had the largest effect on soreness and perceived fatigue (Dupuy 2018, 99 studies; Hou 2026: best early soreness relief, mostly gone by 48 h). [READ]
- **Perform:** 29 trials, 1,012 people: no effect on strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue; a small soreness gain driven by outliers (Davis 2020). No tool beat passive recovery for performance (Jin 2026). A tennis tournament bundle that included massage changed nothing (Wiewelhove 2021). [READ / AGENT]
- **Dose when it was studied:** 5-12 min tended to beat longer (Poppendieck 2016, AGENT). **Feels better is not performs better, even inside one trial** (Duffield 2008, Maia 2026, AGENT). Belief alone can move scores (Broatch 2014, AGENT).

**Certainty:** moderate that massage does not reliably improve next-session performance; the soreness effect is probably real but cannot be blinded.

**Honest line:** fine if you like it and it does not cost you sleep or food. It is comfort, not a recovery lever.

### Q6. "I have matches every day this week. How should I recover between matches?" And: what is the best modality between tennis days?

**Short answer:** the recovery is the routine, not a tool. Between matches on one day: stay warm, eat and drink on the clock. At the end of the day: refuel, rehydrate, protect sleep. Across the week: expect fatigue to stack and add nothing. If you want one physical tool, cold-water immersion after the last match of the day is the best supported, and it is optional.

**Between two matches on the same day (the gap is 2-8 h)**
- Carbohydrate about 1-1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h, small frequent snacks, started at once (Craven 2021, ITF); protein 20-40 g is a bonus that does not speed glycogen. Drink about 125-150% of the weight you lost over 2-5 h, with sodium (ITF, Shirreffs 1996); a meal plus plain water restored balance and a sports drink alone did not in 8 men (Maughan 1996, AGENT). [READ / AGENT]
- Stay warm and re-warm-up about 5 min before the next match (Silva 2018, team sports). In the heat, cool the neck and stay in shade (Lynch 2018, REUSED); heat roughly doubled strength loss and it persisted 24 h (Périard 2014). [READ / REUSED]
- **Do not ice before a match that needs power.** Jump and sprint are lower for the first hours after cold (Zhu 2026, Choo 2023, Berjisian 2026 names a 1-6 h window); the one between-session trial found 738 vs 767 W (Solsona 2023). [READ]
- Mental fatigue builds within a day and peaked at the second same-day match in pro padel (Díaz-García 2021). What helps is thin (section 10b): sleep is the best supported; quiet downtime without screens is extrapolated from lab studies (45 min of phone use worsened vigilance, Jacquet 2023); caffeine for adults if pre-tested (Hornery 2007b). Naps between matches are untested.

**End of the match day**
- Finish the day's carbohydrate (ITF: 6-10 g/kg on double-session days), a protein meal, fluids and salt. Morning check: body mass within 1% and pale urine. Keep alcohol and late caffeine out. Sleep is the priority (Cunha 2023: extension and naps work; a hygiene checklist alone did not change performance). [READ / AGENT]
- Optional tools: cold-water immersion 10-15 min at 10-15 °C (or 5-10 °C); Berjisian calls tournament use "defensible" if it stays away from lifting days. Massage, compression, rolling: comfort. [READ]

**Across the week**
- Expect fatigue to stack (Gescheit 2015, Ojala 2013, Wiewelhove 2021). Add no new training (`training-around-matches`). An easy day after the last match, and heavy leg work waits about two days (practice-based; modest support from Ojala 2013 and Tendero-Ortiz 2024). [READ]

**The "best modality" table (what each tool moves, not an endorsement)**

| Tool | What it moves | What it does not | Certainty | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold-water immersion, 10-15 min at 10-15 °C | Soreness down; a small power gain at 24 h (SMD 0.22 after high-intensity exercise) | Strength; tennis performance in trials; jump is lower for 1-6 h | Low to very low | After the last match of the day. Not between matches. Not routinely after lifting |
| Massage | Soreness and felt fatigue (largest in the comparisons) | Strength, jump, sprint, endurance | Moderate | You like it |
| Compression | Felt fatigue, some soreness | Strength (Négyesi), performance (Jin, moderate-certainty null) | Conflicting | Travel, comfort |
| Active cool-down, 5-15 min easy | Little; blood lactate clears faster | Soreness, next-day performance (mostly) | Low | You like it. Long ones may interfere with glycogen refill |
| Foam rolling | A little pain relief; better as a warm-up | Recovery of performance | Low | Comfort. Percussion guns are unproven |
| Heat (sauna, hot bath) | Mixed; hot packs lowered soreness in non-athletes | Safe default: no, one sauna study hurt next-morning swimming | Low | Not a replacement for cold |
| Stretching | Nothing measurable | Strength, soreness (Afonso 2021) | Very low | Mobility, not recovery |
| Boots, tape, electrical stimulation, anti-inflammatory pills | Trivial or nothing; pills blunt adaptation | | Low | Skip |

**So:** for sport-specific performance, no physical tool has been shown to beat passive recovery. If someone wants one, cold-water immersion after the day's last match has the most consistent small effects. The biggest gains between tennis days are the boring ones: carbohydrate, fluid with sodium, sleep, no extra load.

## Reading order, evidence quality and doses

**Reading order (5 papers, for someone about to write the lesson):**
1. **Duffield 2014**, IJSPP (PMID 23799825). The only tennis trial built on "two sessions in a day". Get the full text for timing and doses before anyone writes "next day".
2. **Wiewelhove 2021**, Eur J Sport Sci (PMID 34075857). The same idea over five tournament days; it found nothing. Read it second, so the lesson never sells Duffield as a protocol.
3. **Gallo-Salazar 2017**, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab (PMID 28231435). Two matches in a day, then sprint, agility, jump and shoulder strength lower the next day in 12 young players. The "why this matters" paper, especially for juniors.
4. **Berjisian 2026**, Sports Med Open (PMC13627639, free full text). The current map of cold-water immersion: what it does and when, the tournament use, the adaptation trade-off.
5. **Reid & Duffield 2014**, Br J Sports Med (PMC3995216, free full text). The best short account of how fatigue builds across match days and why players change their tactics to cope.

Then: **Cunha 2023** (PMID 37462808, free) and **Vitale 2021** (PMID 33607625) for sleep; **Jin 2026** (PMC13531764, free) as the reality check on recovery tools.

**Evidence quality in one paragraph.** Tennis-specific work is a handful of small studies (6-14 players, mostly men, mostly simulated), and padel has almost nothing on physical recovery. The tool literature (cold, compression, massage, active recovery) has many meta-analyses but their certainty is low to very low, nobody can be blinded, and the trials use young men. Soreness and "feeling recovered" are moved far more reliably than next-session performance. Sleep has the best direct tennis data (serve and groundstroke accuracy) but it is one small trial plus extension studies. No mechanism layer was searched; ask if you want one.

**Dose as the studies used it (descriptive, not a prescription):**
- Duffield bundle: 15 min cold-water immersion + 3 h full-body compression + sleep-hygiene advice that night.
- Cold-water immersion: 10-15 min at 10-15 °C (or 5-10 °C) was best across reviews; started within about 15 min of finishing; not routine after every session (Berjisian).
- Sleep: one short night = 5 h or less (Vitale); extension of +1.7 h for a week (Schwartz) or +111 min for 5-7 weeks (Mah); naps 20-90 min, 13:00-16:00, finish 30 min or more before playing (Lastella).
- Massage: 5-12 min tended to beat longer (Poppendieck, AGENT). Compression: about 3 h to 72 h, pressures rarely reported. Active cool-down: 5-15 min, low to moderate (Van Hooren).
- Food and fluid between sessions (gap under 8 h): carbohydrate about 1-1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h, started at once and eaten at least hourly (Craven 2021: 1.02 g/kg/h; ITF 2025, whose running text says 1.0-1.5, so recheck the typeset PDF); protein 20-40 g is a bonus that does not speed glycogen refill; fluid 125-150% of the weight lost over 2-5 h with sodium 40-50 mmol/L; 6-10 g/kg/day carbohydrate on double-session days (ITF).

## 1. The two seed papers (READ)

### Duffield et al. 2013 (print: IJSPP 2014;9(2):273-82)
*Recovery from repeated on-court tennis sessions: combining cold-water immersion, compression, and sleep recovery interventions.*
PMID 23799825 · DOI [10.1123/ijspp.2012-0359](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2012-0359) · randomised crossover.

- **Who:** 8 highly trained tennis players (young men; the record lists adolescent and young adult).
- **Design:** two on-court sessions (drills and match play) in a day, then either the recovery package or a control. Package = **15 min cold-water immersion + 3 h full-body compression garments + sleep-hygiene recommendations that night.** Control = post-session stretching and no sleep rules.
- **Findings, as the abstract states them:**
  - Drill session: stroke and error rates unchanged.
  - Match play after the package: **large effects for more time in play and a higher stroke rate** (d > 0.90).
  - Countermovement jump **tended** to be better before match play (d = 0.90).
  - Heart rate and lactate came down faster after the session, and soreness was lower (d > 1.00).
  - Sleep-hygiene advice increased sleep quantity (d > 2.00) and kept soreness and fatigue lower (d > 2.00; **this is the one effect printed with P < .05**).
- **Caveats to carry into content:**
  - n = 8. The immersion and the compression cannot be separated, so the paper cannot say which one did what.
  - The abstract prints "P > .05" next to most headline effects. It may be a typo for "<", but I have not seen the full text. Lean on the effect sizes, call it small, and check the full text before anyone writes "significant".
  - The abstract says "ensuing" sessions and "2-a-day". Confirm in the full text whether the measured match was the next day before writing "next-day match play".
  - Water temperature is not in the abstract.
  - **A later tennis bundle trial found nothing:** Wiewelhove 2021 (section 3b) gave nine national-level players active recovery + stretching + cold-water immersion + massage across five 2.5-hour matches in five days, and match play, load and recovery were no different from passive recovery. Two tiny bundle trials, two answers. Do not call Duffield "a protocol you can copy".

### Gescheit et al. 2015 (IJSPP 2015;10(7):913-20)
*Consecutive days of prolonged tennis match play: performance, physical, and perceptual responses in trained players.*
PMID 25710259 · DOI [10.1123/ijspp.2014-0329](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2014-0329) · observational.

- **Who and what:** 7 well-trained men played **4-hour matches on 4 consecutive days.**
- **Findings, as the abstract states them:**
  - Movement output ("3D load") fell on days 2 to 4 (effect size -1.46 ± 0.40). Effective playing time fell on days 3 to 4.
  - **Players rated the effort the same (RPE did not differ) while moving less.** The authors link this to pacing and tactical changes.
  - **Post-match 20 m sprint got slower and pre-match countermovement jump got lower on days 2 to 4**, compared with before match 1.
  - **Serve speed held. Serve accuracy after play got worse** (compared with before match 1).
  - Creatine kinase, soreness and fatigue rose every day.
- **Caveats:** 7 men, 4-hour matches (an extreme dose), no recovery intervention tested. "From day 2" is correct for sprint and jump. Serve accuracy is reported post-match against pre-match day 1, not day by day.
- Already used on the site: `articles/pre-competition/training-around-matches.json` (one paragraph) and `Content/parity-drafts/training-around-matches/verify.md`. Context from the repo: Reid & Duffield 2014 review, PMID 24668384 (fatigue may be worse over consecutive days, direct links to match outcome are lacking; REUSED).

### 2b. New from the cold/heat track (2026-10-03)

**What the Berjisian 2026 full text adds (READ, PMC13627639):**
- Sample: 3,210 participants across the 15 reviews, **320 of them female (about 10%)**, nearly all healthy young men from untrained to well-trained.
- **Caution window:** the authors describe CWI as potentially impairing explosive performance in the early post-immersion period, **1-6 hours**. Their words: "Where subsequent high-velocity efforts are required soon after cooling, CWI should be avoided or paired with re-warming and a structured warm-up."
- **Tournaments:** in short-term contexts where immediate recovery outweighs long-term adaptation ("congested fixtures or tournament phases"), targeted CWI "may be defensible", **if it is reserved for technical, tactical or conditioning sessions and kept apart from key resistance sessions.** Endurance benefit is only within about 1 h, and the review "does not support CWI as a strategy to attenuate cumulative endurance decrements across days".
- **Adaptation detail:** strength gains were smaller with leg/limb-only immersion (SMD about -0.31); **whole-body immersion showed no clear effect (about -0.08)**, from the Grgic 2022 subgroup. A Bayesian hypertrophy estimate of -0.22 is cited, but the cold/heat agent could not find that paper. Endurance adaptations looked unaffected.
- **Protocol range:** 10-15 min at 10-15 °C is best for soreness; 5-10 °C for 10-15 min fits CK and jump at 24-48 h. Very long or very cold exposure may impair glycogen restoration (the authors' caution).
- The authors say CWI looks most valuable when recovery time is short and thermal and cardiovascular strain is high (their examples: tournament play, matches on consecutive days, endurance sessions in the heat). Thermal strain was not part of their evidence pool; the claim rests on other work, mostly in hot conditions.

**AGENT (confirmed by the cold/heat agent, not re-opened by me):**

- **Adaptation trade-off in numbers.** Roberts 2015 (PMID 26174323 · 10.1113/JP270570, J Physiol): 21 active men, 12 weeks, twice a week, 10 min CWI vs active recovery; bigger strength and muscle gains with active recovery; type II fibre area +17% vs none. Malta 2021 (PMID 33146851 · 10.1007/s40279-020-01362-0): 8 trials, strength -0.60 (CI -0.87 to -0.33), endurance -0.07. Grgic 2022 (PMID 35068365 · 10.1080/17461391.2022.2033851): 10 trials, strength -0.23 (CI -0.45 to -0.01), whole-body only -0.08 (CI -0.53 to 0.38). Against the trade-off: Gustafsson 2025 (above) and Wilson 2021 (PMID 34324460 · 10.1519/JSC.0000000000004097) found no blunting; Fyfe 2019 (PMID 31513450 · 10.1152/japplphysiol.00127.2019, a training study, not a review): 1RM equal, smaller type II fibre growth; Broatch 2017 (PMID 28679683 · 10.1152/ajpregu.00434.2016): endurance adaptation intact. Muscle biopsies show no anti-inflammatory edge for CWI over active recovery (Peake 2017, PMID 27704555 · 10.1113/JP272881).
- **Dose.** Standard: 10-15 min at 10-15 °C (or 5-10 °C), started within about 15 min of finishing. Match trials: 8-15 °C, 5-15 min, mostly 10 °C for 10 min (Veen). Dose-response is weak: Moore 2023 (PMID 36527593 · 10.1007/s40279-022-01800-1, CWI vs other modalities) found temperature and duration rarely moderate; Wang 2025 network meta-analysis (PMID 40078372 · 10.3389/fphys.2025.1525726): 10-15 min at 5-10 °C for jump; Yu 2026 (PMID 41845491 · 10.1186/s13102-026-01653-5): over 15 min at 5-8 °C for team-sport power, low certainty. The "optimal 11-15 °C for 11-15 min" of Machado 2016 (PMID 26581833 · 10.1007/s40279-015-0431-7) rests on sub-analyses of fewer than 4 studies (per Moore 2022).
- **Several days in a row.** 4 days of 5 × 1 min at 10 °C: less soreness, smaller distance loss (Rowsell 2011, PMID 21077001 · 10.1080/02640414.2010.512640). 5 days of 5 min at about 6 °C: nothing (Krueger 2019, PMID 31797035 · 10.1007/s00421-019-04274-8). Cold beat hot over 5 cycling days (Vaile 2008, PMID 18058595 · 10.1055/s-2007-989267), yet hot equalled cold in Gustafsson.
- **Not supported or conflicting.** Sprint is worse 1 h after CWI (Choo 2023, PMID 36862831 · 10.1080/02640414.2023.2178872). A graded review of 39 post-match football studies backed CWI for perceived recovery only (Querido 2022, PMID 35961644 · 10.1123/ijspp.2022-0038). CWI equalled placebo in Nasser 2023 (PMID 36895634 · 10.3389/fphys.2023.1062398), and beat a thermoneutral bath in Bouchiba 2022 (PMID 36045743 · 10.3389/fphys.2022.860709). Blinding is impossible. Whole-body cryotherapy chamber: Cochrane review, 4 trials, n = 64, very low certainty (Costello 2015, PMID 26383887 · 10.1002/14651858.CD010789.pub2); one study of 12 pro tennis players used -120 °C twice a day for 5 days with unclear allocation (Ziemann 2012, PMID 23182015 · 10.4085/1062-6050-47.6.13). Contrast bathing: Bieuzen 2013 (PMID 23626806 · 10.1371/journal.pone.0062356).
- **Heat is not neutral.** Three 8-minute sauna bouts made next-morning swimming worse (Skorski 2019, PMID 31869820 · 10.1123/ijspp.2019-0333).
- **Gaps (agent's list):** no padel study; tennis data are 9-13 men in simulated matches over 3-5 days; no CWI-alone trial of two sessions in one day in tennis; about 10% female; the 1-6 h window and re-warm-up are untested in tennis.

## 3. Comparing the tools (READ)

- **Dupuy et al. 2018**, Front Physiol. PMID 29755363 · [10.3389/fphys.2018.00403](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00403). 99 studies. Active recovery, massage, compression garments, immersion, contrast water and cryotherapy all lowered soreness (g -2.26 to -0.40). **Massage was the strongest for soreness and perceived fatigue.** Massage and cold exposure were the strongest for inflammation markers. CK, IL-6 and CRP down slightly. These are **soreness and blood markers, not performance.**
- **Hou et al. 2026**, Healthcare (Basel). PMID 42194413 · [10.3390/healthcare14101321](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare14101321) · PMC13206571. Network meta-analysis. Within 24 h: **active recovery and CWI had the largest effects on jump at 24 h (wide confidence intervals)**; CWI and massage helped soreness at 0 and 24 h. By 48-72 h most effects had faded, except CWI still lowering soreness at 48 h. Authors' reading: active recovery for short-term explosive recovery, massage for early soreness, CWI for broader short-term support. A lower-tier journal, so treat the ranking as a hint.
- **Afonso et al. 2021**, Front Physiol. PMID 34025459 · [10.3389/fphys.2021.677581](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.677581). Post-exercise stretching vs rest, 10 RCTs, 229 participants: **no effect on strength recovery (ES -0.08) or on soreness at 24, 48 or 72 h.** Very low certainty, high risk of bias in about 70% of studies. The authors advise against evidence-based recommendations for it. Duffield's control condition was post-session stretching, so the "control" there is itself unproven.
- **CANDIDATE:** Gu 2021, Ann Palliat Med, recovery methods and hormones/blood fatigue markers. PMID 33545755 · 10.21037/apm-20-2409.
- **REUSED:** Davis 2020, PMID 32426160 (massage, 29 studies: small gains in flexibility and soreness, no effect on strength, jump, sprint, endurance or fatigue; from `how-you-get-stronger`). Details in 3b.

Van Hooren (full text, per agent): no gain if the next effort is more than 4 h away; typical dose 5-15 min, low to moderate intensity.
- **Belief.** Broatch 2014 (PMID 24674975 · 10.1249/MSS.0000000000000348): n = 30, a placebo immersion matched cold water on 48 h strength and readiness.
- **Little or nothing.** Stretching (Afonso 2021, section 3). Electrical stimulation (Menezes 2022, PMID 35964921 · 10.1016/j.jpain.2022.05.004, 14 trials). Anti-inflammatory pills: single pre-exercise ibuprofen 800 mg, no benefit, n = 12 (Roberts 2024, PMID 38383211 · 10.1016/j.jsams.2024.02.002); chronic use blunts adaptation (Lundberg 2018, PMID 30102811 · 10.1111/sms.13275). Kinesio tape (Joveini 2024, PMID 39696403 · 10.1186/s13102-024-01032-y): pain SMD -0.44 only after 48 h, all 16 trials at high risk of bias.

**The split (agent's reading, checked against the papers I opened):**
- **Feels better (unblinded):** massage, compression, rolling a little, boots briefly. Percussion guns: unproven.
- **Plays better in the next session:** weak and contested. Compression: yes in Brown, Hill 2014, Li 2025; no in Négyesi and Jin (Négyesi blames older multi-outcome pooling). Active recovery: yes at 24 h in Hou; mostly no in Van Hooren, Jin, Wiewelhove 2021b. Massage: no (Davis).
- **Feels better but does not play better, in the same trial:** Duffield 2008, Maia 2026. Belief alone can move scores (Broatch 2014).
- **Gaps:** no padel recovery-modality trial exists. Tennis has two bundle trials (n = 8 and 9) that cannot separate the parts. Trials mostly use untrained men with lab-induced damage and jump/strength tests, not serve speed, serve accuracy or late-match movement. Blinding is impossible, so belief cannot be separated from physiology.

**AGENT (confirmed in PubMed by the sleep agent, not re-opened by me):**

- **Craven 2022** (PMID 35708888, full text read): 69 papers, 959 healthy adults, 89% men, mostly lab. Overall -7.6% (CI -11.9 to -3.1). By task type: **skill -20.9%**, power -6.3%, endurance -5.6%, strength -2.9%. Worse with early waking or total loss than a late bedtime; afternoon worse than morning; about -0.4% per waking hour. I² 98%, only 8 of 227 tasks after 18:00, 11% women. Use the *skill* point (it fits Vitale and the tennis serve work), never one flat percentage.
- **Naps.** Lastella 2021 (PMID 34194254, full text: 37 studies, 3,489 athletes; naps help most after short sleep; 20-90 min, 13:00-16:00, wait 30 min or more). **Boukhris 2023** (PMID 37700141 · 10.1007/s40279-023-01920-2: 18 studies, 269 mostly male participants; best result was a shuttle test, muscle force not significant; 2 of 18 used polysomnography). **Zheng 2026** (PMID 42558951 · 10.3389/fphys.2026.1878224: 12 RCTs; endurance SMD 0.96, agility 0.55, power n.s.). Waterhouse 2007 (PMID 17852691 · 10.1080/02640410701244983: after 4 h sleep, a 30-min nap at 13:00 gave a 20 m sprint of 3.88 vs 3.97 s). Romyn 2022 (PMID 35606094 · 10.1123/ijspp.2021-0414: wait about an hour after a 1-2 h nap for readiness and agility). Hilditch 2017 (PMID 28366332 · 10.1016/j.sleep.2016.12.016: "30 min or less avoids grogginess" is unreliable). Blanchfield 2018 (PMID 29851569 · 10.1080/17461391.2018.1477180: a 20 min nap before an evening run helped only runners who slept under 7 h). A nap can delay falling asleep that night (13 to 24 min, reported via Lastella).
- **Dose as used:** one short night = 5 h or less (Vitale, Reyner). Extension = +1.7 h for a week (Schwartz), +111 min for 5-7 weeks (Mah), +30% time in bed for 3 nights, about 3% faster time trial (Roberts 2019 MSSE, PMID 31246714 · 10.1249/MSS.0000000000002071). Cunha: +46-113 min and 9-10 h in bed.
- **After a late match.** Nédélec 2015 (PMID 26206724 · 10.1007/s40279-015-0358-z): night matches, bright light, screens, caffeine, alcohol and match arousal. Roberts 2019 above: expect about 80 min less sleep. Evening caffeine of 3-6 mg/kg cut athletes' sleep about 32 min, not significant (Kocak 2025, PMID 41003623 · 10.3390/sports13090317). General-population meta-analyses: caffeine cut sleep about 45 min and should stop 8.8 h or more before bed (Gardiner 2023, PMID 36870101 · 10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764); evening exercise did not hurt sleep unless vigorous and finishing within 1 h of bed (Stutz 2019, PMID 30374942 · 10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0); a warm shower or bath at 40-42.5 °C, 1-2 h before bed helped (Haghayegh 2019, PMID 31102877 · 10.1016/j.smrv.2019.04.008, general population); amber glasses changed felt sleep latency but not actigraphy (Knufinke 2018, PMID 30427265 · 10.1080/17461391.2018.1544278).
- **Why athletes sleep short:** early starts (Sargent 2014, PMID 25222347 · 10.3109/07420528.2014.957306); individual sports about 6.5 h vs 7.0 h in team sports (Lastella 2015, PMID 24993935 · 10.1080/17461391.2014.932016); the Juliff study above.
- **Overhyped or conflicting.**
  - Trackers overestimated sleep against polysomnography (error 85 and 45 min, clinic patients; Budig 2022, PMID 36502241 · 10.3390/s22239540). Athletes' diaries ran 58 min longer than actigraphy (Dunican 2017, PMID 28081034 · 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001697). Marathoners who used trackers reported worse sleep satisfaction, cross-sectional (Cook 2023, PMID 37759947 · 10.3390/brainsci13091346).
  - Pills: no athlete performance trial found; two small melatonin trials disagree (Mahdi 2026, PMID 42124027 · 10.3390/nu18091425; Aykora 2026, PMID 41937599 · 10.1080/07420528.2026.2650828). Caffeine does not replace sleep (Reyner).
  - "Catch up at the weekend": two 9 h nights did not restore attention in sleep-restricted adolescents (Lo 2017, PMID 28364507 · 10.1093/sleep/zsw042); one night of sleep opportunity had a slight and short effect (Banks 2023, PMID 38059385 · 10.1111/jsr.14117); sleep banking is small and not tennis (Arnal 2016, PMID 27015382 · 10.1249/MSS.0000000000000925).
  - Does short sleep for 1-3 nights matter? Walsh 2021 calls it unclear; Craven 2022 and Gong 2024 (PMID 39006249 · 10.2147/NSS.S467531, SMD -0.56) find a clear loss, mostly lab and early-waking designs. A WTA wearable dataset found no link between sleep and performance (Maynard 2025, PMID 41272436 · 10.1177/19417381251387717). These conflict; say so.
  - Phones and devices: Dridi 2025/26 (PMID 41668954 · 10.5114/biolsport.2026.152348) found harm from phone use before bed but with an implausibly large effect; device removal did nothing in other work (Jones 2021, PMID 30741866 · 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002991).
  - Injury links are self-reported associations: under 8 h, OR 1.7 (Milewski 2014, above); chronic sleep loss OR 1.58 (Gao 2019, PMID 30888337 · 10.1097/BPO.0000000000001306); over 8 h, OR 0.39 (von Rosen 2017, PMID 27539373 · 10.1111/sms.12735); none for musculoskeletal symptoms in junior badminton (Skare 2024, PMID 38968530 · 10.1097/MD.0000000000038698).
- **Background reviews:** Fullagar 2015 (PMID 25315456, already used on the site; PMID 25756787 · 10.1123/ijspp.2014-0565), Watson 2017 (PMID 29135639 · 10.1249/JSR.0000000000000418), Bonnar 2018 (PMID 29352373 · 10.1007/s40279-017-0832-x: extension looked best).
- **Evidence gaps (agent's list, and I agree):** no nap or sleep-hygiene trial in tennis or padel, none between two matches on one day; little evening-task or consecutive-night data; women scarce; match results rarely tied to validated sleep measurement; realistic 1-3 h shortfalls untested.

**AGENT (confirmed by the nutrition agent, not re-opened by me):**

- **The ITF statement in full (Vicente-Salar 2025, PMID 40840558)**, read from the author manuscript, not the typeset PDF: for a gap under 8 h, carbohydrate **1-1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h** (the table says 1.2; the **running text says 1.0-1.5**, so check the typeset PDF before quoting), small frequent snacks, protein 20-40 g (0.3-0.6 g/kg), fluid 125-150% of the remaining deficit over 2-5 h with sodium 40-50 mmol/L, **6-10 g/kg/day carbohydrate on double-session days**, pre-match 1-4 g/kg 1-4 h before (the site's existing figure is correct). Morning check: body mass within 1% of baseline, urine specific gravity under 1.020 (quoting Racinais 2015, PMID 26069301). It also notes late-night matches cut sleep and appetite and that a big fluid load late in the evening breaks sleep. The extracted text sits in the scratchpad of this session, not in the repo.
- **Carbohydrate.** Burke 2017 (PMID 27789774 · 10.1152/japplphysiol.00860.2016): about 1 g/kg in the first 4 h optimises refilling; from 4 to 24 h total intake beats type, form and pattern; protein helps only if carbohydrate or energy is short. Ivy 1988 (PMID 3132449 · 10.1152/jappl.1988.64.4.1480): 2 g/kg straight away refilled faster than waiting 2 h (n = 12). Cramer 2015 (PMID 25811308 · 10.1123/ijsnem.2014-0230): fast food worked like sport products (n = 11). Beelen 2010 (PMID 21116024 · 10.1123/ijsnem.20.6.515): if you cannot eat enough carbohydrate, 0.8 g/kg/h carbohydrate plus 0.2-0.4 g protein is about equal to 1.2 g carbohydrate.
- **Protein.** 20 g maxed muscle protein synthesis in one study (Moore 2009, PMID 19056590 · 10.3945/ajcn.2008.26401, n = 6); 40 g beat 20 g (Macnaughton 2016, PMID 27511985 · 10.14814/phy2.12893, n = 30); 4 × 20 g every 3 h beat 8 × 10 g and 2 × 40 g (Areta 2013, PMID 23459753 · 10.1113/jphysiol.2012.244897, n = 24); about 0.4 g/kg per meal (Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, PMID 29497353 · 10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1); position stand 0.25 g/kg or 20-40 g every 3-4 h (Jäger 2017, PMID 28642676 · 10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8). **Before sleep:** 30-45 g casein or whey raised overnight protein balance (Res 2012, PMID 22330017 · 10.1249/MSS.0b013e31824cc363; Trommelen 2018, PMID 28536184 · 10.1152/ajpendo.00273.2016; Trommelen 2023 Sports Med, PMID 36857005 · 10.1007/s40279-023-01822-3) but gave no next-morning strength or CK gain (Ormsbee 2022, PMID 35599912 · 10.1080/15502783.2022.2036451, n = 18); a 2025 trial found a small positive (Bayrakdaroğlu, PMID 41470882 · 10.3390/nu17243938).
- **Fluid and sodium.** Up to 150% of the loss if the gap is under 4 h (NATA, McDermott 2017, PMID 28985128 · 10.4085/1062-6050-52.9.02). Sodium at least 50 mmol/L (Maughan & Shirreffs 1997, PMID 9232555 · 10.1080/026404197367308) or the ITF's 40-50, against about 20 in typical sports drinks. A **meal plus plain water at 150% restored balance; a sports drink alone left the players 337-373 mL short at 6 h** (Maughan 1996, PMID 8781863 · 10.1007/BF02425493, n = 8). Low-fat milk halved urine output compared with water (Shirreffs 2007, PMID 17459189 · 10.1017/S0007114507695543, n = 11). The ACSM 2007 abstract (Sawka, PMID 17277604) gives no percentage, so cite the ITF, NATA or Shirreffs instead.
- **Tennis sweat and sodium.** Pros start matches hypohydrated (urine SG 1.022 in Hornery; 1.026 in López-Samanes 2018, PMID 29624621 · 10.1371/journal.pone.0195242). Sweat about 1.1 ± 0.4 L/h (0.3-2.0) and sweat sodium about 38 ± 12 mmol/L, roughly 1.1 g per 68-min match with a range of 0.5-1.8 g (Lott 2011, PMID 22089307 · 10.1123/ijsnem.21.6.492, indoor at 17 °C). All men, small groups.
- **Not supported, mixed or harmful.**
  - Protein-heavy refuelling: adds nothing to glycogen when carbohydrate is on target (Craven; Witard 2025, PMID 40117058 · 10.1007/s40279-025-02203-8). In 75 RCTs every significant effect came from trials that did not match energy (Zhao 2025, PMID 41433039 · 10.1080/15502783.2025.2605338). No consistent soreness or function benefit (Pasiakos 2014, PMID 24435468 · 10.1007/s40279-013-0137-7).
  - Vitamin C and E: 1,000 mg C + 235 mg E blunted mitochondrial adaptation (Paulsen 2014, PMID 24492839 · 10.1113/jphysiol.2013.267419, n = 54); 1 g vitamin C delayed strength recovery (Close 2006, PMID 16611389 · 10.1079/bjn20061732). Skip.
  - BCAA: small CK and soreness effect, no performance recovery (Salem 2024, PMID 38241335 · 10.1080/27697061.2023.2297899); effects needed over 200 mg/kg/day for more than 10 days (Fouré 2017, PMID 28934166 · 10.3390/nu9101047).
  - Omega-3: soreness effect below the clinical threshold (Lv 2020, PMID 32382573 · 10.1155/2020/8062017); needs at least 2 g/day for 6 weeks or more (Li 2026, PMID 41891174 · 10.1096/fj.202504783R). Curcumin: meta-analyses disagree (Beba 2022, PMID 35574627 · 10.1002/ptr.7477; Oxley 2023, PMID 37408367 · 10.1177/02601060231186439).
  - Anti-inflammatory pills and alcohol: 1,200 mg ibuprofen a day blunted strength and muscle growth (Lilja 2018, PMID 28834248 · 10.1111/apha.12948); kidney injury 52% vs 34% in an 80-km race (Lipman 2017, PMID 28679502 · 10.1136/emermed-2016-206353; not significant, OR 2.1, CI 0.9-5.1). Alcohol at 1.5 g/kg cut muscle protein synthesis by 24-37% even with protein (Parr 2014, PMID 24533082 · 10.1371/journal.pone.0088384, n = 8).
  - Caffeine for refuelling: glycogen +66% at 8 mg/kg (Pedersen 2008, PMID 18467543 · 10.1152/japplphysiol.01121.2007, n = 7), but 400 mg six hours before bed disrupted sleep (Drake 2013, PMID 24235903), so daytime only.
  - Chocolate milk and tart cherry: see the table; optional extras at best.
  - Low energy availability: plausible in a tournament week but never measured across a tennis or padel tournament (the ITF says studies are lacking). Elite tournament energy use of 3,400-4,600 kcal a day (Ellis 2023, PMID 36965488 · 10.1123/ijspp.2022-0297); the RED-S model is contested (Jeukendrup 2024, PMID 39287777 · 10.1007/s40279-024-02108-y).
  - IOC supplements statement (Maughan 2018, PMID 29540367 · 10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027): "more research is needed" for most of these.
- **Gaps (agent's list):** no tennis or padel muscle-biopsy glycogen data; no test of whether faster refuelling changes the second match (carbohydrate in 2 h 40 min of simulated tennis did not change skills: Hornery 2007b, PMID 19171960 · 10.1123/ijspp.2.4.423); no recovery-nutrition trial in a multi-day tennis event (the five-day trial tested physical recovery methods only, Wiewelhove 2021). Padel: no sweat, sodium, glycogen or recovery-nutrition study, only a diet-and-supplement survey (Toro-Román 2023, PMID 37630822 · 10.3390/nu15163633), hydration habits in 12 men over 65 (Lopes 2024, PMID 39458506 · 10.3390/nu16203513) and the mental-fatigue study already listed. Women are only 4-9% of the glycogen participants, and the ITF text is narrative; its top protein dose rests on one isotope study.

- **Díaz-García 2021**, PMID 34501648 · 10.3390/ijerph18179059: 14 professional padel players; mental fatigue rose after matches and built up across two matches in one day. The only padel recovery-adjacent study on the site so far.

- **Gabbett 2016** PMID 26758673 and **Soligard 2016** PMID 27535989: sudden load spikes and crowded calendars and injury risk.
- **Kellmann 2018** PMID 29345524 (stress-recovery balance) and **Meeusen 2013** PMID 23247672 (functional overreaching vs overtraining; other causes for a slump).

**AGENT (confirmed by the tennis/padel agent, not re-opened by me):**

- **Real draws vs lab marathons.** Professionals held serve speed in five-set Wimbledon matches (Maquirriain 2016, PMID 26960753 · 10.1080/17461391.2016.1156163) and showed no jump or grip loss after a clay-tournament match (López-Samanes 2018, PMID 29624621 · 10.1371/journal.pone.0195242). The likely reason is dose: 4-hour simulations on four days vs real draws about two days apart (Nosaka 2024, PMID 39086854 · 10.3389/fspor.2024.1422986).
- **Heat and sprint.** Girard 2014, two papers from the same heat study (PMID 24668378 · 10.1136/bjsports-2013-093165; PMID 24668381 · 10.1136/bjsports-2013-093286): sprint, jump and leg-stiffness losses of 1-6% were gone by 24 h in both conditions. Hornery 2007 Sports Med review (PMID 17326696 · 10.2165/00007256-200737030-00002): fatigue decrements in long or hot matches were not convincing in that review.
- **Juniors.** 80-minute matches changed nothing (Colomar 2022, PMID 35719231 · 10.52082/jssm.2022.245). Workload spikes and injury: shoulder complaints rose 1.26 times per spike in 301 players (Johansson 2021, PMID 34693828 · 10.1177/19417381211051643); an acute:chronic ratio of 1.3 or more is flagged in a 2025 review (Amor-Salamanca, PMID 41150471 · 10.3390/sports13100336). No overreaching in 12-14-year-old camps (Żurek 2022, PMID 35329172 · 10.3390/ijerph19063486).
- **Padel.** Marcos-Rivero 2025 (PMID 40942670 · 10.3390/s25175240): 52 amateurs, 13 matches, jump not lower. HRV in padel was studied only inside matches (Parraca 2022, PMID 35329317 · 10.3390/ijerph19063623). The agent searched PubMed for padel creatine kinase and found nothing, and found no padel soreness or strength time-course, no multi-day physical recovery study, no recovery trial and no workload-injury study. Courel-Ibáñez, Sánchez-Alcaraz, Escudero-Tena, Pradas and Carrasco-Páez all publish on demands, fitness and game analysis, not recovery.
- **Tennis monitoring.** Most tennis HRV work is about pre-match anxiety, not recovery tracking (Villafaina 2022, PMID 36671710 · 10.3390/biology12010017). There is no tennis recovery consensus; the nearest is the IOC load statement (Soligard 2016, already listed).
- **Seed note:** PubMed holds no Gescheit hormonal or inflammatory follow-up; the 2016 technical paper is the follow-up.

**How long recovery takes, by outcome (the agent's summary, checked against the abstracts above):**
- **One 2-3 hour match:** sprint, jump and leg-stiffness are back by about 24 h on average, with some players still down (Gomes, Périard/Girard). Knee-extensor strength is down 9-10% straight after in cool conditions and **about 22% in the heat, and the heat loss was still there at 24 h** (Périard).
- **Two matches in one day:** still lower the **next day** in young players, in sprint, agility, jump and shoulder strength (Gallo-Salazar 2017).
- **Three 2-hour matches in three days:** leg force and rate of force development are not recovered 1-2 days after the last match, and CK and soreness are still up after a rest day (Ojala). In collegiate women after a tournament, markers were near baseline at +1 day and better at +2 (Tendero-Ortiz).
- **Soreness and CK:** raised 24-48 h after a 3-hour match, build over consecutive days (Gomes, Gescheit 2015, Ojala, Poignard).
- **Technique and mood:** slip on days 2-4 of 4-hour matches (Gescheit 2016), but not in the real-draw studies above.
- **Gaps:** tiny samples (6-14), mostly men, simulated; one women's study (n = 9); junior double-match data are boys only; nothing measured past 48 h; no real-tournament series; the match-length dose-response is unmapped (1.5 h and 4 h disagree); jump looks insensitive (Ojala).

## 7. Angles that could carry content (my suggestions, each tied to a source above)

1. **Ice after the last session of the day, not between two sessions.** Zhu 2026, Xiao 2023 and Berjisian 2026 all find the jump is lower right after immersion and better by 24-48 h, and Berjisian names a 1-6 h caution window. The one direct test of ice between two sessions found slightly lower power 1.5 h later (738 vs 767 W, Solsona 2023, 12 elite skaters, small effect). For a double day the sensible reading is: if the second session needs power, skip it or re-warm well; if it is the last of the day, the next morning is where it may help. (Inference from pooled data and one non-tennis trial, not tested on tennis double days.)
2. **Feels recovered is not the same as plays recovered.** Cooling and massage reliably lower soreness; objective performance effects are small or absent (Hohenauer, Davis, Dupuy). Duffield is the rare tennis result that also shows more time in play, from 8 players and a bundled package.
3. **Sleep is the part of the package that needs no kit, but be exact about what it buys.** In Duffield the sleep advice raised sleep and kept soreness and fatigue lower (the one effect printed P < .05). The later trials add a limit: hygiene plans reliably lengthen sleep (Lever 2020 in junior tournaments, Fullagar 2016 after a late match) without a measurable gain in match outcomes or recovery markers. Sleep *extension* and *naps* are what Cunha 2023 found most effective. So: "protect the hours" is well supported, "a hygiene checklist makes you recover faster" is not.
3b. **Short sleep hits the serve before it hits the legs.** One night of 5 h or less: serve and groundstroke accuracy fell 14-24%, repeated sprints did not (Vitale 2021, 12 junior boys). Craven 2022 agrees in direction (skill tasks fell most). Strong reel hook, said with "one small trial".
3c. **A night match costs about 80 minutes of sleep** (Roberts 2019, observational, elite athletes of all sports) and the morning after a padel tournament exit, pros reported worse sleep, mental toughness and reaction time (Díaz-García 2023, n = 23, self-report). That is the double-day and next-day-match problem in one number.
3d. **Naps are the evidence-backed rescue for a short night** (20-90 min, early afternoon, finish 30-60 min before you play) but they are untested between two tennis matches. Say so.
3e. **Your sleep tracker is not a recovery score.** Trackers over-read sleep against lab measurement (Budig 2022 and Dunican 2017, AGENT).
4. **By day 3 the body protects itself by moving less (Gescheit).** Same RPE, less movement; serve speed holds while accuracy falls. A good opener for a tournament-week piece, with the 4-hour caveat said out loud.
5. **Training block vs tournament week.** The same ice bath is a different decision in each (Roberts, Malta, Berjisian). Berjisian calls tournament use "defensible" if it stays away from the lifting sessions. Matches the site's current "skip after lifting, optional after matches" line. One nuance for accuracy: the strength penalty is clearest for leg-only immersion, and whole-body immersion showed no clear penalty in the Grgic subgroup. The site line can stay as the cautious version, but do not claim "ice baths kill gains" as settled.
5b. **In tennis, ice changes how sore you feel; on-court numbers barely move.** Poignard 2023 (13 players, three match-like days): soreness climbed only with passive recovery, performance held in all groups. Wiewelhove 2021 (section 3b): no change in match play across five days. The fatigue the trials create is mild, so "no difference" does not prove "no use".
6. **Heat is the missing half, and it is not a safe alternative.** Wang 2021: hot packs lowered soreness longer than cold, but in non-athletes and for pain only. Ahokas 2025: 14 trials of sauna or hot-water immersion, acute effects mixed (4 none, 4 helped, 1 harmed). Three sauna bouts hurt next-morning swimming (Skorski 2019, AGENT). A footnote: no tennis or padel trial exists.
7. **Cold reaches the outer muscle only** (Freitag 2021): a short, visual reel fact.
8. **Stretching as a recovery tool is unproven** (Afonso 2021), yet it is the usual "cool-down" and was Duffield's control.
9. **Two tiny tennis bundle trials, two answers.** Duffield (n = 8, two-a-day) saw more time in play the next session; Wiewelhove (n = 9, five 2.5-hour matches in five days) saw no difference from doing nothing, while fatigue still built up in both conditions. Honest framing: nobody has shown which part works, or whether any does, in tennis, and padel has no trial at all.
10. **The best-supported answer is a sham.** In the endurance network meta-analysis a fake immersion ranked as high as the real tools (Jin 2026), and a placebo immersion matched cold water on strength at 48 h (Broatch 2014, AGENT). Good for a "belief is real, but it is not physiology" segment, said gently: if a routine you like helps you settle, it is allowed to help. It just should not cost you sleep or food.
11. **Cooling down on the court: a short easy cool-down does little for tomorrow and can slow glycogen refill** (Van Hooren 2018). Not a reason to stop moving; a reason not to believe it replaces the next steps (food, fluids, sleep).
12. **Two matches in a day cost you the next day** (Gallo-Salazar 2017: sprint, agility, jump and shoulder strength all lower the day after, 12 young players). A strong "why this matters" opener for juniors and their parents. The follow-up (2019): the second match is run at the same heart rate but felt harder.
13. **How long do you really need?** One normal match: legs mostly back in about a day, some players not (Gomes, Périard). Several matches in a row: force and speed qualities lag 1-2 days after the last match and one rest day was not enough (Ojala, n = 8). Collegiate women were near baseline at +1 day and better at +2 (Tendero-Ortiz). The site's "48 hours after a match before the main sessions" is practice-based; this is modest support for it, not proof. Say "an easy day after, and be careful with heavy leg work for two".
14. **Heat doubles the strength you lose** (Périard 2014: about 22% vs 9%, still present at 24 h) and ties the recovery piece to the existing `hydration-and-heat` article.
15. **Real draws are kinder than lab marathons.** Gescheit's four 4-hour days are an extreme. Real pro matches did not always show losses (Maquirriain, López-Samanes, AGENT). The honest story is "it depends on how long you played and how many days in a row".
16. **Padel: say what we don't know.** Match load sits near doubles tennis and below singles (Armstrong 2023). Single padel matches did not lower jump or grip (Carnero-Diaz 2024, Marcos-Rivero 2025), though one lab study did (Armstrong). What does show up in padel is mental fatigue that builds within a day (peaking at the second match, apparently reset overnight), and after a multi-day event worse sleep, mental toughness and reaction time (Díaz-García 2021, 2023). Nobody has measured how long padel players take to recover physically.
17. **Carbohydrate is the between-session tool with a measured effect on the thing that matters.** Refilling muscle fuel is the one recovery process with a clear dose in athletes: about 1 g per kg of body weight per hour, started at once, at least hourly (Craven 2021), and adding protein did not speed it. For a player with a second session 3-4 hours away this is a stronger, cheaper lever than anything in sections 2b-3b. (Caveat: nobody has shown in tennis or padel that faster refuelling changes the second match.)
18. **Players start matches already short of water** (Hornery 2007 in BJSM: urine specific gravity 1.022 before play; López-Samanes 2018, AGENT) and a sports drink alone left players short at 6 h while a meal plus water restored balance (Maughan 1996, AGENT, n = 8). Rehydration is a "drink more than you lost, with salt, over a few hours" job, tied to the existing `hydration-and-heat` article.
19. **The night between match days:** finish the day's carbohydrate (ITF: 6-10 g/kg a day on double-session days), put fluids and salty food earlier in the evening, a protein meal or 30-45 g of casein before bed is reasonable for overnight muscle repair but is not proven to help the next day's play, and alcohol or late caffeine cost you sleep and repair (Parr 2014, Drake 2013, AGENT).
20. **The supplement shelf is mostly noise.** Tart cherry is small and mixed, BCAA, omega-3 and curcumin are weak or inconsistent, vitamin C and E can blunt adaptation, and anti-inflammatory pills blunt it too (section 5b). Good reel material in the "what I would not spend money on" format, with the honest note that some of it is untested in racket sports.

## Do not say

- "Ice baths speed up recovery." The honest version: lower soreness for up to 24-96 h, a small power benefit at 24-48 h, no strength benefit, and a cost to training adaptations if used after every session.
- "Duffield proved ice, compression and sleep work." It is 8 players, a bundle, and an abstract with mostly non-significant P values.
- "Tournament play drops your serve speed." Serve speed held in Gescheit. Accuracy fell.
- "4 days of tennis will do X to you." Those were 4-hour matches, 7 men.
- "CK drops with ice." It did not survive the bias adjustment in Zhu 2026.
- Any single-percentage claim from Craven 2022 or any "nearly 2 h" for Schwartz & Simon.
- "A sleep-hygiene routine makes you recover faster." Hygiene lengthened sleep in two trials without improving recovery or match outcomes (Fullagar 2016, Lever 2020).
- "Short sleep ruins your sprint." In Vitale 2021 repeated sprints held; accuracy fell.
- "Nap between matches." Nobody has tested it in tennis or padel.
- "Compression sleeves/tights make you recover faster." One meta-analysis says yes for strength, a newer one and a network meta-analysis say no (Négyesi 2022; Jin 2026, the one moderate-certainty finding). They make legs feel better.
- "Massage restores your legs for the next match." It lowers soreness and perceived fatigue. It did not change strength, jump, sprint or endurance (Davis 2020).
- "A cool-down jog speeds recovery." Largely ineffective for next-day performance and can interfere with glycogen refill (Van Hooren 2018).
- "Recovery guns / boots / tape / stretching / e-stim work." Unproven or unsupported (section 3b).
- "The tennis research proves a recovery protocol." The tennis recovery-tool evidence is two bundle trials of 8 and 9 players that disagree.
- "Ice baths kill your gains." Say what was found: regular use right after lifting gave smaller strength and muscle gains in several trials, clearest with leg-only immersion; whole-body immersion and some newer trials showed no penalty; endurance adaptation looks unaffected. It is a reason to keep ice away from lifting days, not a verdict.
- "Ice restores your strength or your sprint." Strength recovery shows up in one soccer meta-analysis whose prediction intervals include zero; sprint did not improve in any of them.
- "Ice before your second session." Sprint and jump are lower for the first hours after cold (Choo 2023, Zhu 2026, Berjisian), and the only between-session trial found 4% less power (Solsona 2023).
- "Hot baths or sauna are the safe alternative." Evidence is mixed and one sauna study hurt next-morning performance.
- "You need 48 hours." No tennis study tests a 48-hour rule. Say "the day after is easy, and heavy legs wait" and keep it labelled as coaching practice.
- "A tennis match wrecks you for days." After one normal match legs were back in about a day on average (some players not), and real pro draws often showed no loss. The multi-day losses come from long, repeated, simulated matches in small groups.
- Anything numeric about how long padel players take to recover. There is no study.
- Gescheit 2015/2016 as "what a tournament does to you". Seven men, four consecutive 4-hour matches.
- "Have a protein shake right after your match to refuel." For glycogen, adding protein to enough carbohydrate did not speed refilling (Craven 2021). Protein matters for the day's total and for muscle repair; it is not what gets you ready for the next hit.
- "Drink 1.5 L per kg lost" as one fixed number. The ITF range is 125-150% of the deficit over 2-5 h with sodium; the trial evidence says "more than you lost, with enough salt".
- "20-40 g of protein is the most your body can use." One isotope trial found a bigger, longer response to 100 g than 25 g (Trommelen 2023). It is "enough for most people", not a cap, and it does not show better performance.
- "A sports drink rehydrates you." Not alone: in one small trial a meal plus plain water did the job and a sports drink alone did not (Maughan 1996, AGENT).
- Any nutrition claim for padel recovery. There is no padel sweat, glycogen or recovery-nutrition study.
- Tart cherry, BCAA, omega-3, curcumin, vitamin C or E as recovery tools. Mixed, small or counterproductive (section 5b).
- Anything from Berjisian 2026 without saying its certainty is low to very low.

## 10. Why and how tennis players get tired: the mechanism layer

Asked 2026-10-03: why players get tired, how, what factors, and what is affected (physical, cognitive, technical, tactical). All three parts are merged (10a physical, 10b mental and emotional, 10c technical and tactical) and 10d puts them in one plain-language map. Labels as above. Evidence tags used by the researchers: **match** = played match (tournament or organised), **lab** = lab or field protocol, **borrowed** = from another sport, **speculative**.

### 10a. Physical and physiological (physical-mechanism track, 2026-10-03)

**The framework (READ).** Enoka & Duchateau 2016, Med Sci Sports Exerc 48(11):2228-38. PMID 27015386 · [10.1249/MSS.0000000000000929](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000929) · PMC5035715. Fatigue is a disabling symptom produced by the interaction of **performance fatigability** (a measurable drop in what muscles and nervous system can do) and **perceived fatigability** (how hard it feels: effort, temperature, thirst, pain, motivation). The authors argue against sticking labels like "central" or "mental" on fatigue itself and for finding the actual changes behind it. That gives the two-box picture used in 10d: your body can do less, and it feels harder.

**What a match demands (READ + AGENT).**
- **Pluim 2023** (Sports Med 53(4):807-36, PMID 36752978 · [10.1007/s40279-022-01807-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01807-8); meta-analysis, 64 articles): rally about 5.5 s for international men (6.4 s women), about 4 strokes per rally, **effective playing time 18.6% (men) and 20% (women)**; running 9.6 m per point and about 2.3 km per best-of-5 men's match (1.2 km women's); first serve 182 km/h men, 156 women.
- **Giles 2024** (J Strength Cond Res 38(3):517-25, PMID 38320234 · [10.1519/JSC.0000000000003937](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003937); 182 Australian Open matches, Hawk-Eye): **1.6 changes of direction per point, one every 2.7 s for men (3.1 s women)**, about two thirds of them sharper than 105°; men did high-intensity changes about twice as often as women.
- **Martin 2011** (J Strength Cond Res 25(1):163-70, PMID 21157392 · [10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181fb459b](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181fb459b); 6 matches, 6 high-level players): **clay vs resin: heart rate 154 vs 141, lactate 5.7 vs 3.6 mmol/L, rally 8.5 vs 5.9 s, effective play 26 vs 19.5%.** Running speed tests did not slow during the matches.
- AGENT: rest between points about 16-25 s (work:rest about 1:2 to 1:5), heart rate about 140-160, lactate about 2-4 mmol/L, average oxygen use about 51% of maximum with peaks near 87% (refs 36752978, 11404666, 17237121, 18979340, 17326695). The profile is prolonged moderate intensity with bursts; the phosphagen system fuels single points and aerobic metabolism supports the recovery between them.

**The mechanisms, by how well they are shown in tennis (E = established in tennis, B = borrowed, S = speculative):**
1. **The legs lose force, partly because the nervous system drives them less, partly at the muscle (E).** Girard 2008 (J Electromyogr Kinesiol 18(6):1038-46, PMID 17611122 · [10.1016/j.jelekin.2007.05.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2007.05.005), READ; 12 trained players, a 3-hour match): knee-extensor maximal force **-9%**, muscle activity (normalised) down, the low- to high-frequency stimulation ratio **-12%**, while twitch and electrical-wave measures did not change. The authors name central activation failure and altered excitation-contraction coupling. Girard 2010 (Scand J Med Sci Sports 21(3):455-64, PMID 20030784 · [10.1111/j.1600-0838.2009.01032.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2009.01032.x), READ; calf muscles, 3-hour protocol): lower force went with reduced activation and about 80% lower spinal reflex excitability. Larger central loss in the heat (Périard 2014, READ). The cell-level story (potassium, calcium release; lactate and acidity minor) is borrowed (18195089, 16195008). Borrowed from cyclists: core temperature near 40 °C and low blood glucose also lower muscle activation (11509498, 12673141); tennis core temperature reaches about 38.5-39.4 °C.
2. **Stop-start loading with mild muscle damage (E).** The direction changes above (Giles) are the eccentric, braking work. Adolescents make about 0.6 accelerations and 0.6 decelerations (≥2 m/s²) per minute (25111161, AGENT). CK and soreness rise mildly (Gomes 2014, Ojala 2013). The eccentric-damage mechanism is borrowed (14715039); the idea that stretch-shortening actions explain why tennis losses are smaller than in continuous exercise is partly the authors' reading (Girard 2006).
3. **Heat and hydration (E, modest).** Mass loss in matches is small (0.3-1.05%), but players start hypohydrated (Hornery 2007 in BJSM; López-Samanes 2018). Heat roughly doubled strength loss (-22% vs -9% cool, Périard 2014). In 37 °C an individualised drinking plan lowered heart rate about 11 beats and thermal strain but not strength loss (24668383, AGENT). The "2% body-mass loss" threshold for performance is borrowed and rarely reached in tennis (20689090).
4. **Fuel: blood glucose is shown, glycogen is not measured in tennis (E for glucose, B for glycogen).** Ferrauti 2003 (J Sci Med Sport 6(1):28-39, PMID 12801208 · [10.1016/s1440-2440(03)80006-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1440-2440(03)80006-3), READ): **64% of 147 tournament players reported hypoglycaemic symptoms**, most often **in the warm-up for the second match of a day**, where blood glucose fell from 5.8 to 4.3 mmol/L (tournament) and 5.4 to 4.1 (practice); the authors advise precautions for the early stage of a second match. **No muscle-biopsy glycogen study exists in tennis or padel** (the physical agent searched). Soccer shows nearly half of fibres almost empty (16775559, borrowed). Reid & Duffield call glycogen or glucose "likely" decisive without tennis data. Carbohydrate trials are mixed: it kept stroke quality after about 2 h in two studies (Vergauwen 1998, 9710871; McRae 2012, 22248499) and did nothing in 3-hour simulated matches in two others (Gomes 2013, 24148197; Hornery 2007b).
5. **Perceived fatigability rises on its own track (E).** Girard 2006 (Br J Sports Med 40(6):521-6, PMID 16720888 · [10.1136/bjsm.2005.023754](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2005.023754), READ; 12 players, 3 h): force -9% and leg stiffness -9% (not significant), **jump power held until after the match**, heart rate 144, effective play 21%, **effort rating and soreness rose in a straight line** (r = 0.99). Over several days players moved less at the same effort rating (Gescheit 2015).
6. **Hormones, nerves and heart-rate variability are not validated fatigue markers (S).** Cortisol rises with the match and with dehydration (r = -0.63, López-Samanes), is blunted by carbohydrate (23860288) and did not predict who won (Ojala). A pre-match HRV drop reflects nerves (35508281); HRV did not change 1-2 days after a collegiate women's tournament (Tendero-Ortiz 2024).

**Modifiers (AGENT unless marked):** match length and repetition (Ojala, Gescheit, Gallo-Salazar); **surface (clay costs more, Martin 2011, READ)**; heat; fitness (the same activity produces the same oxygen use and heart rate in advanced and recreational veterans, 19197208); age and sex (adolescents make fewer high-intensity accelerations, 26694104; children resist fatigue better and women are usually less fatigable in isometric tasks, borrowed 17123327, 24433272); sleep (section 4b); previous-day load.

**Upper body (READ + AGENT).** Moreno-Pérez 2019 (PLoS One 14(4):e0215015, PMID 30978212 · [10.1371/journal.pone.0215015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0215015); 26 pros, one 80-minute match): dominant-shoulder external-rotation strength **-4.8%**, internal-rotation range -1.3% (external range up 5.3%), **serve speed -1.2% (not significant)**; the authors recommend post-match recovery for the shoulder. AGENT: scapular rotation changes of 2-4° after a serve protocol recovered by 24 h (27434703); chest and wrist-flexor strength -13% and -8% and serve speed -4.5% after strenuous tennis (24239164); forearm grip -15% only with stiff strings (24603658); in juniors the external rotators fatigue more than the internal (69% vs 83% fatigue ratio, 10342564).

### 10b. Mental and emotional (mental-fatigue track, 2026-10-03)

**What mental fatigue is.** A state brought on by long periods of demanding thinking (sustained attention, decisions, inhibiting impulses) that makes effort feel harder and attention, reaction and decisions worse, without the muscles failing. In studies it is induced by 30-90 minutes of a Stroop-type task. [AGENT, borrowed + racket sport]

**AGENT (confirmed by the mental-fatigue agent, not re-opened by me):**

- **Effort feels harder; the body is not failing.** Mental fatigue shortened time to exhaustion with higher perceived effort and unchanged heart rate, lactate and muscle output (PMIDs 19131473, 23698244, 28044281); racket sports show the same (Ding). The brain-level story (prefrontal and cingulate load, dopamine, adenosine; 32176398, 29923147) is a hypothesis, and the adenosine idea is disputed (32661840). Glutamate built up in a prefrontal region after a workday of control tasks and choices drifted to low effort (35961314). A 3-hour vigilance task raised reaction time and misses (15913965).
- **The effect on physical performance is contested.** Brown 2020 meta-analysis (PMID 31873926, 73 studies, 2,581 people): endurance g = -0.38; a bias-sensitive re-analysis (Holgado, 33103052) gave 0.08, not significant; a preregistered replication (32519588, n = 30) found nothing for endurance; the large ego-depletion replication found d = 0.04 (27474142). Strength, power and anaerobic effects trivial. So "mental fatigue kills endurance" is overstated; the case is stronger for **skill, accuracy, reaction and decisions**, though samples are small (10-66, mostly men) and effect sizes are probably inflated by publication bias.
- **Time course.** After a 32-min task, felt fatigue partly recovered within 20 min while brain-wave and movement-time measures were still impaired (33488457, n = 15). Overnight rest reset it in padel (above).
- **Sleep loss and travel feed in.** Beach-volleyball players' sleep restriction slowed reactions at least as much as mental fatigue (+147 vs +79 ms; 40417017). Sleep loss lowers positive mood and raises anxiety (Palmer 2023, 38127505, meta-analysis of 154 studies, not athletes). Pro tennis players report poor sleep and stress-linked sleep reactivity (40329801). WTA players slept 11 min less per time zone crossed on the first night, with no link to results (41272436); long-haul performance effects are inconclusive (36287181).
- **Stress and emotion.** Cortisol rises before a first-round match; losers had higher cortisol and cognitive anxiety (18845399, 25251448), but cortisol did not predict results over a 3-day event (Ojala 2013). No tennis study tested emotion regulation under mental fatigue. Thirst worsened Stroop-induced fatigue (33212138, n = 15).
- **Physical fatigue and thinking.** Groundstroke accuracy fell 40-50% after high-intensity but not moderate fatigue (Lyons 2013, section 10c) and 69% at volitional fatigue (Davey 2002, 12003276). Decision-related movement control degrades only near exhaustion (29902127); expert badminton players anticipated worse under high load (30931825); one review found little objective evidence of exercise-induced mental lapses (21425889). Dehydration: a small cognitive cost (ES -0.21, attention -0.52; Wittbrodt 2018, 29933347) against a null meta-analysis (30876770); heat hurts psychomotor and perceptual tasks more than "cognitive" ones (17915603).

**What helps, what is overhyped (all evidence thin):**
- **Sleep** is the best-supported and the only tennis-specific lever (Reyner & Horne 2013; section 4b). A hygiene programme lengthened junior players' sleep at tournaments without changing results (Lever 2020).
- **Caffeine**: moderate, mostly borrowed. About +14% time to exhaustion after mental fatigue in 8 people (27695980); in simulated tennis, 3 mg/kg kept final-set serve speed (Hornery 2007b, IJSPP, READ). Never tested for accuracy or decisions under mental fatigue, and no substitute for sleep. Under-18 stance on the site: no caffeine for playing.
- **Fatigue inoculation** (thinking blocks inside training): promising in padel (Díaz-García 2023, READ; a combined mental plus physical warm-up beat a physical one under sleep loss, 40149756, n = 31). One lab group.
- **Weak or mixed:** carbohydrate mouth rinse (32751162, 38065422, 34439591); carbohydrate in tennis lowered cortisol but not performance (Hornery 2007b; 23860288); rest breaks (31725384); naps (36518294, not specific to mental fatigue); mindfulness (28664327; one tennis pilot, 31548907; 90 table-tennis players with lower fatigue and cortisol, 41721510); music (26305353). Slow breathing raises vagal HRV (35623448), but nobody showed it reverses mental fatigue. A 2022 review of 33 countermeasure studies (Proost, 35543922) found caffeine, odours, music and extrinsic motivation best supported, with the mechanism speculative.
- **Smartphones:** 45 min of phone use worsened vigilance and inhibition (Jacquet 2023, READ). Daily 30 min of social media before training blunted gains in swimmers and volleyball decisions in a few studies from one lab (32930640, 34404292, 36028977) but not strength or jump (37024109). Phone use before bed worsened soccer players' sleep and reactions (41668954). **Nothing is tested in tennis or padel.** The researcher could not find a "Pageaux 2020" smartphone-before-exercise paper and found that Smith 2018 (29623604) is a soccer review, so do not cite those for the phone point.
- **Overhyped:** "willpower as a fuel", "mental fatigue is a proven endurance killer", glucose or adenosine "fixes".

**Padel:** all the padel data come from one group (Díaz-García 2021 n = 14, 2023 n = 23, 2023 RCT n = 61, 2025 n = 31). Known: matches tire the mind, motivation and reaction fall, sleep and mental toughness suffer across an event. Unknown: shot selection, in-match accuracy, partner communication, women-only data, and a control day that separates mental from physical fatigue.

**Gaps:** no tennis or padel test of anticipation or tactical decisions under mental fatigue; fatigue is induced by Stroop tasks, not by matches; nothing separates mental from physical fatigue inside a match; recovery time course is 20 min (lab) and overnight (padel, n = 6); emotion regulation, decision density and time pressure are unstudied in tennis; countermeasures untested in racket sports; replication and publication-bias problems.

### 10c. Technical and tactical (technical-tactical track, 2026-10-03)

**What gets worse, in order of how strong the evidence is:**

1. **Serve speed: small and uneven in real matches.** Flat from set 1 to set 5 in 15 Wimbledon five-setters (Maquirriain 2016, PMID 26960753, reused). Mean first-serve speed down about 2.4% by set 5 in 146 hard-court Grand Slam matches (Cui 2020, READ). Down about 4% in one 3-hour match in 8 advanced men (Martin 2016, READ). In lab simulations of 2 h 40 min, serve and groundstroke velocity fell (Hornery 2007b, IJSPP, READ).
2. **Accuracy and margin go first, mostly at high intensity.** Groundstroke accuracy equalled rest at 70% of peak heart rate and fell 40-50% at 90% (Lyons 2013, READ). Technique was steady below 80% of VO2max and fell above it: ball speed -9 to -13%, accuracy -18 to -19% (Brechbuhl 2017, READ). In played matches, serve accuracy after play was worse while speed held (Gescheit 2015), with more unforced errors and lower second-serve winning (Gescheit 2016). First-serve percentage stayed flat in pros (Maquirriain; Cui).
3. **Lab fatigue overshoots a match.** Run-to-exhaustion protocols push far above match intensity; match play sits at about 73-81% of peak heart rate (Davey 2003, 12846533). Match-induced fatigue is more realistic but uncontrolled, because tactics change too. Hornery's 2007 review found no convincing overt decrement in long matches (17326696).
4. **Mechanics (one 3-hour match).** Ball speed, impact height, maximal joint velocities (wrist flexion about -14%, shoulder internal rotation about -7.5%, trunk about -5%) and joint forces fell; **the timing of the movements stayed the same** (Martin 2016, READ). Leg muscle activity fell 10-40% on first serves while speed held, which looks like compensation (Hansen 2024, 39305469). No data on groundstroke kinematics or racket-head speed.
5. **Footwork.** Turn-sprint about 1% slower, repeated sprint 2-3% slower, leg stiffness -6.5%, all restored within 24 h after a shorter match (Girard 2014, 24668378). Players moved less at the same effort rating over several days (Gescheit 2015). **Split step and reaction to the ball under fatigue are unstudied** (in rested players a split step speeds the first step about 13%, Nieminen 2013, 23600926, so any fatigue cost is speculation). Eccentric strength explained 25-37% of change-of-direction time in basketball and tennis players (Smajla 2022, 35646491). Hawk-Eye work: higher peak speed into end-range moves went with lower shot quality (Armstrong 2025, 39808394).
6. **Tactics (interpretive).** In late sets of Grand Slam matches: more winners and net success, fewer serve winners; the authors name fatigue, pacing, risk and opponent familiarity and the data cannot separate them (Cui 2020). More "stretch" groundstrokes and less effective playing time over four days (Gescheit 2016). In the heat: slower pace, longer matches, more double faults (Simani 2026, 42788661, 13,081 Grand Slam matches) and fewer net approaches (Smith 2017, 28919493). **Players tired of the legs seem to change how they play** (Reid & Duffield 2014); deliberately shortening points or taking more risk when tired is speculation.

- **Does fatigue cost matches? Not shown directly.** The agent ran eight targeted PubMed searches and found no study tying previous-match duration, rest days or scheduling to the next result. Reid & Duffield: causal links from physiological loss to match results are lacking; players seem to adjust tactics instead. The stroke-level cost in matches is small and sits in margin shots (second serve, unforced errors, stretch returns); whether it flips results is speculation. Winners varied less set to set (Cui; association only). The best-documented cost is availability: more cumulative load, more time lost (Kovalchik).
- **Injury link.** Associations: juniors, shoulder complaints rose 1.26 times per workload spike (Johansson 2021, 34693828) and back pain 1.17 (34693813); acute load linked to injury but a poor predictor (Moreno-Pérez 2020, 32877321); WTA pros, 56.6 injuries per 1,000 match hours (12.8 time-loss), lower limb 51%, match-load measures associated with injury (Dakic 2017, 29074474, 52 players); an acute:chronic load ratio of 1.3 or more in juniors and 1.5 or more in adults is the strongest extrinsic predictor in a 2025 review (41150471). **"Tired technique then altered mechanics then injury" is not shown:** fatigue-protocol reviews find force and proprioception fall but mechanics are inconsistent (Jildeh 2019, 31903400; Barber-Westin 2017, 28298066, not racket sports); in the 3-hour match joint forces fell as players tired (Martin); serving after training lowered speed, accuracy and shoulder strength/ROM in 25 youth elites, with the injury link the authors' speculation (Fernandez-Fernandez 2020, 33396257). Muscle fatigue is listed as a risk factor at low-to-moderate quality (32316156); shoulder risk factors are limited or conflicting (Asker 2018, 29581141). Sites: shoulder, elbow, lumbar spine, lower limb (51% in WTA pros). No tennis study isolates ankle or knee under fatigue.
- **Padel.** Winner-minus-error gap widened in the last set and last round in 532 WPT matches (Escudero-Tena 2021, 34064529). Physical loss in single matches small or inconsistent (Marcos-Rivero 2025, 40942670; Carnero-Diaz 2024 jumps rose; Armstrong 2023 jumps fell). Injury sites: elbow, knee, shoulder, low back (Dahmen 2023, 37337550: 3 per 1,000 training hours, 8 per 1,000 match hours); 24.1% of a Portuguese self-report sample blamed fatigue or overload (41011098). No study of shot choice, net play, wall use or rally length against fatigue.
- **Note on one agent line:** it wrote that mental fatigue "accumulated over three matches in two days" in padel. The correct wording is in 10b: it accumulated between the two matches on day 1 and did not rise further at the third.

**Gaps:** nothing ties previous-match duration, rest or scheduling to the next result; no design separates fatigue from tactics and opponent adaptation; women, juniors and long matches are sparse and match-play studies are small (15 matches or fewer); groundstroke kinematics, return, split step and reaction are unstudied under fatigue; tired technique causing injury is unproven in tennis and unstudied in padel; padel has no fatigue-tactics study and its matches (about 1 hour) probably do not mirror 3-hour tennis.

### 10d. The map in one picture (my synthesis of 10a-10c and the earlier sections, in plain language)

**Why a tennis player gets tired.** A match is one to three hours of short bursts: rallies of 5-8 seconds, only about 20% of the clock spent actually playing, a change of direction every 3 seconds or so, each one a brake and a push, at a moderate average heart rate (140-160) and low lactate (Pluim 2023, Giles 2024, Girard 2006). It does not look like a sprint, so players rarely run out of breath. What wears them down is the repetition (thousands of brakings and pushes), a decision on every point, and often heat and a fuel tank that dips.

**How it happens: two things at once** (Enoka & Duchateau 2016):
1. **The body can do less.** After a 3-hour match leg force is about 9% lower, partly because the nervous system drives the muscle less and partly at the muscle itself (Girard 2008, 2010). Heat roughly doubles that loss (22% vs 9%, Périard 2014). Dominant-shoulder rotation strength is about 5% lower after 80 minutes (Moreno-Pérez 2019). The braking work leaves mild damage that peaks at 24-48 h (CK, soreness).
2. **It feels harder.** Effort rating and soreness climb in a straight line through the match, before jump power drops (Girard 2006), and thirst, heat and mental load add to it.

**What decides how bad it gets:** how long you played and how many matches or days in a row (Gescheit, Ojala, Gallo-Salazar); heat and the hydration you started with (pros start dehydrated, Hornery in BJSM); fuel (blood glucose dips in the warm-up of a second match of the day, Ferrauti 2003); surface (clay costs more, Martin 2011); sleep (Vitale 2021); fitness, age and sex (thin data); and what you did in the days before (load spikes, Johansson 2021).

**What gets worse, in order of how strong the tennis evidence is:**

| Area | What changes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Leg force and how fast it is produced, change-of-direction and sprint, jump a little later; shoulder rotation strength; soreness; movement output across days | Strong for tennis (small studies, mostly simulated) |
| Cognitive | Attention, reaction time, motivation, and after hard thinking second-serve and groundstroke accuracy; composure on day 3-4; decisions (borrowed from other sports) | Moderate for tennis and padel, small samples; effect sizes contested |
| Technical | **Accuracy and margin go before speed** (second serve, depth, unforced errors); serve speed barely moves (about 2-4%); the timing of the movement is kept; large losses near exhaustion, small in real matches | Moderate; lab larger than match |
| Tactical | Late sets: fewer serve winners, more winners and net; more stretch shots; less movement at the same effort over days; heat: slower pace, more double faults. Whether players choose this or are forced into it is unknown | Weak (descriptive) |
| Results | Not shown directly. The best-documented cost is availability (more cumulative load, more time lost) | Gap |

**What is not shown** (so it has to be said carefully): that fatigue changes match results; that tired technique causes injury; muscle-glycogen depletion in tennis; the physical recovery time course in padel; anything past 48 hours after a single match.

**The best way to recover, by what failed** (each row cites the sections above; extras are comfort or borrowed):

| What failed | Best-supported recovery | Evidence level | Optional extras |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leg force, soreness | Time (one match: mostly back in about a day; several matches or days in a row: 48 h and more), sleep, eating enough, no extra leg or braking load | Trials of the time course plus consensus | Cold water, massage, compression: soreness and comfort, not performance |
| Fuel and fluid | Carbohydrate about 1-1.2 g/kg/h for 4 h when the next session is under 8 h away, fluids 125-150% of what you lost with sodium, and eat before the warm-up of the second match of the day | One meta-analysis and trials plus consensus | |
| Heat | Start hydrated, shade, cool the neck at changeovers | Moderate | Cold water afterwards if heat strain was high (borrowed) |

| Shoulder | Keep rotator strength up in the gym and keep serve volume sensible; scapular changes were gone by 24 h in studies | Weak | |

**So the honest one-line answer to "why do I get tired and how do I recover?"** Because hundreds of brakes and pushes, a decision every point, heat and a dipping fuel tank pile up, and they make your legs able to do less and everything feel harder. The recovery that is best supported is giving it time, food, fluid and sleep; the tools are extras.

## 11. What happens if you don't recover: consequences, warning signs, how much is enough

Answer to Q2, from the consequences track (2026-10-03). Tags: **shown in tennis**, **shown in other sports**, **association only**, **speculative**.

### 11a. The consequences, and how well each one is shown

1. **You play worse, and it feels harder, across consecutive days.** Shown in tennis (short simulated tournaments, mostly men): Gescheit 2015, Ojala 2013. Over weeks, in 7 national junior girls: load up 161% then 55%, win ratio fell, stress scores rose (Filaire 2008, PMID 18651370; uncontrolled, AGENT).
2. **One short night costs accuracy.** Shown in tennis (Vitale 2021; section 4b).
3. **More injuries after load spikes.** Association only. Back pain 1.17 times per spike (Johansson 2021, 198 juniors, READ). Injury 1.62 times per extra 1,860 arbitrary load units in the latest week, but a poor predictor, n = 15 (Moreno-Pérez 2020, PMID 32877321, AGENT). **No trial shows that extra rest prevents injury.**
4. **More infections.** Association in tennis (17 elite women, weekly respiratory infections followed training and competition load; saliva immunoglobulin did not predict them, Novas 2003, PMID 12740744, AGENT). The sleep-to-colds link is shown experimentally in non-athletes (Prather 2015, READ; Cohen 2009, PMID 19139325: under 7 h, 2.9 times the odds, AGENT).
5. **Staying flat for weeks.** Shown in other sports by self-report (Matos 2011, READ). No tennis prevalence.
6. **Burnout and quitting (juniors).** Association only. High burnout risk in 55% of girls and 16% of boys aged 8-15 in one cross-sectional survey (Peraita-Costa 2020, PMID 32028574). In youth table tennis, players who later quit scored worse on devaluation and motivation 6 years earlier (Martinent 2018, PMID 29764338); better recovery scores predicted fewer "reduced accomplishment" symptoms later (Martinent 2022, PMID 36571013). Recovery is one input beside motivation, perfectionism and pressure (Lemyre 2008, PMID 17617173; Garinger 2018, PMID 30220213). All AGENT.
7. **Under-fuelling in heavy blocks.** Shown in a small tennis camp (Fleming 2022, READ). The ITF statement covers low energy availability (reused).

**Speculative:** low-grade inflammation with low ferritin and high hepcidin after a tournament season (Ziemann 2013, PMID 24149803); the 4.8% loss of dominant-shoulder external-rotation strength after one match has no recovery follow-up (Moreno-Pérez 2019, READ).

**Counter-evidence:** planned hard blocks followed by rest raised fitness in pros (Fernandez-Fernandez 2015, PMID 26664275; Thiel 2011, PMID 23487475) and planned junior camps did not cause overreaching in 12-14-year-olds (Żurek 2022, PMID 35329172). All AGENT.

### 11b. Early warning signs and simple monitoring

- **The reviews list symptoms, not validated early-warning tools** (Kreher 2012). The one early sign it names (full text, agent): a higher effort rating for the same work. Commonly listed symptoms: heavy flat legs, starting a session but not finishing it, poor or unrefreshing sleep, irritability or low mood, lost motivation, repeated colds or niggles, a slump that outlasts a rest week. None of this has been validated in tennis.
- **What monitoring evidence there is.** Subjective measures beat objective ones (Saw 2016, READ; the IOC agrees). In endurance athletes, effort ratings, heart-rate response and mood questionnaires flagged functional overreaching while HRV did not, and one mood scale could not tell it apart from normal fatigue (Roete 2021, PMID 34108275); resting HRV barely moves with overreaching (Bellenger 2016, PMID 26888648); resting-heart-rate shifts are small against day-to-day noise (Bosquet 2008, PMID 18308872); in the one tennis case study (3 pros, 30 days) HRV fell 13-64% in players who were getting fitter (Thiel 2011). All AGENT.
- **Tennis:** no study validates HRV or a wellness score for decisions. The questionnaire and mood work is 7-12 girls (PMIDs 18651370, 16586341, 23200107).
- **Practical idea, untested in tennis (my suggestion):** a one-minute daily rating of sleep, energy, mood, soreness and motivation plus session effort, read as a 1-2 week trend against the player's own baseline. It fits what Saw 2016 found, but nobody has tested it in tennis.

### 11c. How much recovery is enough

- **Studied.** One rest day did not restore leg force after three 2-hour matches in three days (Ojala 2013). Recovering from overreaching takes about 2 weeks (Halson 2004, PMID 15571428), non-functional overreaching weeks to months (Kreher 2012, Matos 2011). A 1997 expert review of endurance sport says a weekly rest day with hard-easy alternation lets heavy load be tolerated and risk rises near 3 weeks of intensified training (Lehmann 1997, PMID 9190120; opinion). AGENT.
- **Consensus for children (not trials).** The AAP 2007 report as summarised by the American Academy of Family Physicians (aafp.org, not PubMed; the agent fetched it): **at least 1 day a week off all organised sport, 2-3 months a year off any one sport, 5 days a week or fewer per sport, weekly increases of 10% or less.** The AAP 2024 update (Brenner & Watson, READ above) exists; **its text could not be opened, so check the numbers there before quoting them.** A junior tennis review (Thurber 2025, PMID 41272920, low level of evidence) suggests 12 hours a week or less of organised tennis, no more than 2 tournaments a month, and links more than 40 matches a year to injury; more hours a week than age in years went with serious overuse injury (OR 2.07, Jayanthi 2015, PMID 25646361, multi-sport). AGENT.
- **Coaching practice only (no study found):** days off after a tournament, how often to lighten a week, rest days between matches. No tennis or padel trial tests any rest prescription.

### 11d. Junior points for parents

- About 3 in 10 young athletes have had a multi-week slump, and training load did not predict it (Matos 2011, READ). In 272 Swedish age-group athletes 37% reported being "stale" and 41% lost motivation; stress outside training and relationships mattered (Kenttä 2001, PMID 11531041, AGENT).
- **Overuse is the main injury problem.** In elite 11-14-year-olds overuse problems were present in 12% of weeks and illness in 6% (Pluim 2015, PMID 25944058); the injury rate was 12.9 per 1,000 hours in under-16s against 3.4-4.6 in under-10 to under-14 (O'Connor 2020, PMID 31928289); risk markers were a previous injury and more than 6 hours a week (Hjelm 2012, PMID 20561286; Oosterhoff 2018, PMID 29912622). AGENT.
- **Sleep.** More than 8 h on weekdays went with 0.39 times the injury odds (von Rosen 2017, PMID 27539373; mixed sports, association). A sleep programme added sleep at tournaments but did not change results (Lever 2020, READ).
- **Food.** 92% of 24 adolescent girl players met disordered-eating or low-energy-availability criteria, but so did 71% of controls (Coelho 2013, PMID 23318655), so the screen is broad. AGENT.
- **Burnout.** Junior tennis players who burned out had less say in training and perceived more parental criticism and expectations (Gould 1996, quoted second-hand in Jayanthi 2013, PMID 24427397; Gould is not in PubMed). Early specialisation: injury OR 1.27, serious overuse OR 1.36 (Jayanthi 2015). AGENT.

### 11e. Padel

No padel study covers overreaching, multi-day recovery, sleep and performance, or load spikes. Only cross-sectional self-report injury surveys exist: 2.8-4.0 per 1,000 hours (Declève 2025, PMID 40644841, 457 recreational players, median age 42, 36.5% injured; Ferreira 2025, PMID 41011098; Meyer 2025, PMID 40677135); players blamed fatigue or overload for 24% of injuries (Ferreira); insomnia was associated with injury (Declève); World Padel Tour injury rates were 10.5 (men) and 15.1 (women) per 1,000 matches (Pérez 2023, PMID 37413954). A 57-minute amateur match produced no jump or grip decline (Marcos-Rivero 2025), so any recovery problem would come from several matches a day or consecutive days, which nobody has studied. Borrow the tennis evidence and say so. All AGENT.

### 11f. Gaps

No prevalence of non-functional overreaching or overtraining in tennis or padel; multi-day tennis studies are 7-12 players, mostly men, 3-5 days; injury-and-load work uses small samples, self-reported injuries and has weak prediction, and nothing tests rest as prevention; no tennis study by consecutive day or match number (tournament data are format and round associations); no tennis-specific Olympic or Grand Slam illness figures were retrieved; Gould 1996 is not in PubMed; "Cohen 2015" in my brief is Cohen 2009, and Prather 2015 has Cohen as senior author; most papers were read as abstracts.

## 12. Supplements for recovery: the evidence behind Q4

From the supplements track (2026-10-03). All PubMed items were read as abstracts unless marked. ### 12a. Frameworks

- **AIS** (Burke et al. 2026, Int J Sports Med, PMID 42309155 · [10.1055/a-2891-6315](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2891-6315), READ abstract): a "food first" programme that grades supplements A-D by strength of evidence, safety and anti-doping risk, with batch testing. - **IOC 2018** (Maughan, PMID 29540367): supplements are a minor contribution and diet is assessed first; good evidence only for caffeine, creatine, buffers and nitrate, all performance aids. **ISSN:** creatine may aid recovery (Kreider 2017, PMID 28615996); caffeine is a performance aid (Guest 2021, PMID 33388079). **ACSM/AND/DC 2016** (Thomas, PMID 26891166): only the abstract was reachable; nutrition aids recovery, refer to a dietitian, no supplement list.
- **Youth:** SDA 2014 (Desbrow, PMID 24668620, READ): core foods rather than supplements; check calcium, vitamin D, iron. AAP 2005 (Gomez, PMID 15805399): virtually no efficacy or safety data in minors. AAP 2011 (PMID 21624882): energy-drink caffeine has no place in youth diets.

### 12b. Do you need a supplement at all? (the ladder)

1. **Name the problem.** Check sleep, energy eaten, carbohydrate and protein after sessions, fluid and salt, and load. Junior academy 14-year-olds in a camp ate 2,526 kcal against 3,959 spent and slept 6.9 h (Fleming 2022, READ).
2. **Can food do it?** Carbohydrate about 1 g/kg early if the next match is soon, 20-40 g protein, fluid with sodium (ITF manuscript lines 979-999). If yes, stop.
3. **A diagnosed gap?** Blood test and a clinician first (IOC; Peeling 2023, PMID 37358750). Iron, vitamin D, calcium, B12 for vegans. Time-limited, then retest.
4. **A practical gap?** Travel, no kitchen, no appetite, a short turnaround: use a food-format product. Powders must be batch-tested (Informed Sport, NSF Certified for Sport, Cologne List; ITF lines 1018-1024).
5. **Under 18:** steps 1-4 only, with a parent and a doctor or dietitian. No performance supplements.
6. **Adults only, and not recovery:** caffeine 3-6 mg/kg, creatine in a strength block. Test in training first (IOC).

### 12d. Cramps (a very common tennis complaint)

The cause is unresolved and probably mixed.
- **Sodium and dehydration camp.** Bergeron 2003 (PMID 12801207) and Kovacs 2006 (PMID 24198676) are tennis reviews that name sodium loss as a main driver in heat; sweat can exceed 2.5 L/h (agent).
- **Altered neuromuscular control camp.** Schwellnus 2009 (PMID 18981039, READ): the sodium and dehydration case rests on anecdote, 18 case-series cases and one small (n = 10) case-control study; four prospective cohorts do not support it; altered neuromuscular control (a spinal-reflex problem in fatigued muscle) is better supported. Troyer 2020 (PMID 32720102, a tennis review, agent): fatigue matters more than electrolytes, but electrolytes may matter for recurrent whole-body cramps. **Maughan & Shirreffs 2019** (PMID 31696455 · [10.1007/s40279-019-01162-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01162-1), READ): both mechanisms probably occur, and "none [of the folk remedies] is consistently effective"; the search for a single strategy is unlikely to succeed.
- **Trials.** Jung 2005 (PMID 15970952, READ; 13 cramp-prone men, 37 °C): a carbohydrate-electrolyte drink delayed the first cramp (36.8 vs 14.6 min in the 7 who cramped both times), yet 9 of 13 still cramped, so hydration and electrolytes are not the only cause. Miller 2010 (PMID 19997012, READ; lab-induced cramps in hypohydrated men): a small amount of pickle juice shortened a cramp by about 49 s compared with water, which the authors attribute to a mouth reflex rather than electrolytes. Vitošević 2025 (PMID 40863778, agent) rates the pickle-juice evidence as limited. **Magnesium fails** (Garrison 2020).
- **ITF** (lines 1274-1278): sodium-forward plans in heat are sensible, and the link is "not without controversy".
- **Net:** salty food or drink in long hot matches is low-risk and plausible; a pickle-juice sip is a weak acute trick; magnesium pills are unsupported; fatigue and building up load gradually are probably the bigger levers. All as suggestion, not instruction.

### 12e. Tennis and padel specifics

- **Surveys.** ITF (lines 1006-1013): 80-100% of elite players use at least one supplement, mainly for recovery and energy. Kondric 2013 (PMID 24149808): almost all women and 80% of men; coaches were the main advisers. López-Samanes 2017 (PMID 29280665, 71 pros): 81% use, sports drinks 82%; top-100 players use more caffeine, creatine, iron and carbohydrate-protein mixes; dietitians advise the top 100, coaches and S&C staff the rest. Toro-Román 2023 (PMID 37630822, 123 padel players): creatine about 15% of men, vitamin complexes about 10% of women; lower-level players' diets do not match demands.
- **Trials (all performance, small, mostly men).** Caffeine raised serve speed and sprints (Hornery 2007b, n = 12; READ); **3 mg/kg of caffeine in an energy drink improved handgrip, high-intensity running and sprint count in 14 elite 16-year-olds, with no change in serve ball speed (Gallo-Salazar 2015, PMID 25158287 · [10.1123/ijspp.2014-0103](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2014-0103), READ)**; a 6% carbohydrate drink did nothing in a 2 h 40 min simulated match (Hornery 2007b); creatine had no effect (Pluim 2006; Op 't Eijnde 2001); bicarbonate kept stroke consistency (Wu 2010). Vicente-Salar 2020 (PMID 32957505, READ): 21 racquet-sport RCTs, 15 of them tennis and 1 padel; acute caffeine 3-6 mg/kg 30-60 min before improves specific skills and accuracy but may not change perceived exertion; creatine, bicarbonate, citrate, beetroot, citrulline and glycerol need more studies.
- **Recovery supplements.** The agent's searches found no recovery-supplement RCT in tennis or padel.

### 12f. What this does to the site's current wording (suggestions for Amir to decide; nothing changed)

The site's stance (`articles/nutrition/training-nutrition`): "I don't recommend any pills, powders or supplements here. If a doctor prescribes iron or vitamin D after a blood test, that's the doctor's call." and "Under 18: no caffeine for playing and no energy drinks. At any age: food, not pills and powders. Some supplements can harm your health or contain banned substances."
- **Keep:** food first and the under-18 line. SDA, AAP and the ITF (creatine not recommended for juniors, manuscript lines 1905-1906, in the wheelchair section) all back it. The juniors' real gap is food and sleep (Fleming 2022).
- **Footnote for adults, convenience and travel.** "At any age: food, not pills and powders" is stricter than any framework: the AIS Group A, IOC and ITF (lines 594-599, 1037-1040) accept batch-tested drinks, powders and liquid meals when food is impractical. Possible line: "Adults: if you truly cannot eat enough after a late match or on the road, a batch-tested drink or powder is a tool. It is not a recovery drug."
- **Footnote for a diagnosed deficiency.** Keep the doctor line, add that the test comes first. This is where pills have clear evidence (iron, vitamin D, B12). A nudge toward a vitamin D test fits both audiences, since the UK and the Middle East had the highest inadequacy (Farrokhyar 2015).
- **Tighten (1):** name the "don't bother" list: BCAA, magnesium, high-dose vitamin C/E, melatonin gummies, pre-workouts. Reviews conflict (Heaton 2017, PMID 28702900, with authors linked to a sports-drink institute, lists antioxidants as recovery aids; Paulsen 2014 says they blunt adaptation).
- **Tighten (2):** quantify contamination and date it. 94 of 634 products (14.8%) carried undeclared steroids, bought in 2000-01 (Geyer 2004, READ); reviews report 12-58% (Martínez-Sanz 2017, PMID 28976928); certified batch testing lowers the risk but is unevenly available (Wardenaar 2025, PMID 41468235).
- **Tighten (3):** caffeine under 18 is a coaching rule, not a toxicity line. EFSA 2015 sees no safety concern up to 3 mg/kg/day for adolescents (web summary, not opened); 3 mg/kg helped 14 elite 16-year-olds (Gallo-Salazar 2015, READ). Keep "no", and give the honest reasons: sleep (Drake 2013), no safety data for repeated performance use in minors (AAP 2005), energy drinks (AAP 2011; ITF).

### 12g. Gaps

No recovery-supplement RCT in tennis or padel (padel has one survey and one RCT in the whole racquet literature); youth data are tiny (caffeine n = 14, melatonin n = 10, creatine safety 5 studies); contamination figures are old and there are no Iran-specific supplement-market data; not read: the ACSM/AND/DC full text (403), the Cohen 2023 numbers, the AIS Group D page (search summary only), the IOC details (web summary); the ITF junior-creatine line is expert opinion, not a trial; several reviews have industry-linked authors (Heaton 2017; ISSN stands).

**AGENT (confirmed by the night-before agent, not re-opened by me):**
- ITF manuscript numbers: day before 6-10 g/kg per 24 h with lighter training; loading 10-12 g/kg for 36-48 h only if practical for possible five-set matches; pre-match meal 1-4 g/kg at 1-4 h; morning check body mass within 1% and urine specific gravity under 1.020; 5-7 mL/kg about 4 h before; sodium aids retention; big evening fluid loads cost sleep; junior intakes often below target (PMID 40840558). Burke 2017 (PMID 27789774) and the ACSM position stand (PMID 26891166, abstract carries no gram figures). Loading in children: a review found not enough evidence (PMID 19337125). Past gut trouble predicts symptoms (r 0.37-0.51, n = 221; PMID 21775906); 1.1 L of fluid immediately before running raised stomach pain.
- Hydration and cognition: pros arrive at specific gravity 1.022 (Hornery) and 1.026 (López-Samanes); in boys aged 14, pre-play specific gravity tracked end-of-match core temperature (n = 8, PMID 17562747); a 2.7% mass loss slowed 5-10 m sprints but not serve or groundstrokes (Magal 2003, n = 11, PMID 12544649); cognition ES -0.21, attention -0.52, larger above 2% (PMID 29933347).
- Sleep: six nights at 9.8 vs 8.2 h in bed raised time to exhaustion 3.9% (12 men, PMID 27250809); sleep banking (Arnal 2016, PMID 27015382); Schwartz & Simon 2015 (PMID 26325012); Bonnar 2018 review (PMID 29352373); Walsh 2021 (PMID 33144349). Clock-watching raised worry and onset time even in good sleepers (n = 60, PMID 16793001). E-reader (Chang 2015, PMID 25535358). Delayed bedtime with normal waking was not consistently harmful; deprivation and early waking were; afternoon and evening tasks suffer more than morning (Craven 2022). Sprint and power are spared (Fullagar 2015, PMID 25315456).
- Calm and routine: any of four strategies about 1 h before a match cut anxiety in 100 elite juniors (PMID 8532469, no pure control); slow breathing under 10 breaths a minute lowers arousal (PMID 30245619); five minutes a day of cyclic sighing for a month beat mindfulness for mood (PMID 36630953); an imagery task shortened sleep onset in 41 insomniacs (PMID 11863237). No pre-performance-routine trial in tennis was found.
- Caffeine and alcohol: Gardiner 2023 (PMID 36870101: 24 studies, -45 min sleep, -7% efficiency, +9 min onset); Drake 2013 (PMID 24235903); alcohol fragments the second half of the night and cuts REM (PMID 23347102).
- Avoid list: "just in case" ibuprofen (PMID 20424410); sedatives (PMID 36147967); melatonin gummies (content -83% to +478% of label in 31 products, PMID 27855744); tracker scores (PMID 33378539); Miall 2018 gut training (PMID 28508559, n = 18); pre-match HRV not significantly lower in 11 ITF juniors (Villafaina 2022, PMID 36671710).
- Tournament and travel: evening starts cut sleep about 80 min (Roberts 2019, PMID 30217831); a new bed's first night is worse, then recovers (PMID 27112296); bring familiar snacks (Ranchordas 2013, PMID 24149799); earplugs and an eye mask (ITF quoting Walsh).
- Padel: amateur matches 57 ± 16 min, 152 ± 40 points, heart-rate load rising each set, no jump or grip decline (Marcos-Rivero 2025, PMID 40942670); padel vs doubles tennis similar heart rate and lactate (n = 8, PMID 40606278); morning-after-elimination sleep, mental toughness and reaction time worse (Díaz-García 2023); sleep-deprived padel players (n = 31) did worse on a padel test and Stroop (PMID 40149756).

**Gaps:** no tennis or padel study links night-before food, fluid or sleep to a match result, and all sleep-performance data are accuracy tests in 12-17 players; glycogen across a match is unmeasured; the AASM teen sleep range is not in the retrieved abstract (it was confirmed on aasm.org earlier, `sleep-and-recovery/verify.md`); no pre-performance-routine trial in tennis; no night-before stress-routine trial; nothing on hotel food safety, evening fluid and night-time bathroom trips, illness and fever rules, teen caffeine dosing, or competitive padel hydration and sleep; the JAMA 2023 melatonin-gummy paper (PMID 37097362) has no PubMed abstract and was not used.
