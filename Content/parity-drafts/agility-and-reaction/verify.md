## (c) LESSON FIXES (TPS lesson and its English twin)
1. **Split step timing.** The lesson says "time your split step so you land just as the cue arrives". The data say otherwise. In Uzu 2009, landing well before the usual response time gave 7.8% misses against 0.8% when landing near it. World-class returners land when early ball-flight information is available, after the server's contact (Mecheri 2019). Better wording: "land as you start to pick up the cue, not before".
2. **"Reaction training improved agility but not planned change of direction."** The only support is one 7-week trial of small games (not reaction drills) in 25 U18 Australian footballers (Young & Rogers 2014), and a 2026 meta-analysis of speed and agility training found no significant effect on reactive agility. Rewrite as: one small trial in another sport found small games cut decision time while change of direction drills did not help. Do not state it as a general finding.
3. **"Reaction does not improve from strength alone"** is an absence of evidence, not a finding. Reactive strength (drop jump) linked to agility in one small study. Say "no study shows ...", and keep that one study in.
4. **The four levels (planned, simple, semi-reactive, reactive), the 2 to 3 to 4 option ladder and the 16-week progression** are practice-based. The lesson presents them as fact. Label them as the course's own grading.
5. **Under 16 versus 16 and over** (cue at split step only below 16, "taste of real reaction" in weeks 14 and 15): no study supports an age cutoff for reactive cues. It is a safety judgement. Mark as a coaching rule.
6. **"Players quickly learn to guess"** and the even/odd-second rule are practice-based. No paper tests that rule, and evenly paced reps can make it predictable.
7. Keep the lesson's honest limit ("small studies, other sports"). It is correct, and the article repeats it.
