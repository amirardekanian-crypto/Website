Exercise Card Blueprint

An athlete with no video gets three short cues today, and nothing else. The fix is one new thing on the Spine, How to do it: a few plain steps, written once for everyone. Then each part of the card gets one job, and nothing is said twice. Tested on 12 real exercises with a simulated beginner reading the words alone, confidence went from 2.3 to 3.6 out of 5.

3 · How to write each layer

The writing rules

These are what you asked for: what's best for the cues, how to instruct so no video is enough, what a pill should add, and what a Coach's Note should hold.

Spine · for everyone · new field "steps"

How to do it

The test: someone who has never seen the exercise, with no video, can do it safely from these words alone. That's the international standard for describing an exercise (CERT item 8: "described well enough to replicate").

  1. Set up: the kit, where you and the weight go, and the normal way to hold it ("a dumbbell in each hand, palms facing you"). A grip pill on the card overrides it (Q3).
  2. Start position.
  3. The move, ending at something you can see or feel ("until your back knee is just above the floor"), never "go deep".
  4. The way back, and where the rep ends.
  5. Optional: breathing, putting a free weight down safely, or "do all the reps on one side, then switch".

3–5 steps (2–3 for a stretch or the bike; the count follows the entry's pattern so two writers agree). One phase per step, under 90 characters, "you" voice, everyday words. Gym words get a plain version: "push your hips back" instead of "hinge", "the muscles under your armpit" instead of "lats". The entry's own name is never jargon.

What the pilot added, from a test reader with no video:

  • Every direction word needs its reference. "Side-on", "square", "sit back", "caving in" and "middle toes" each caused a guess or a wrong picture. "Hips face the floor", "level with your shoulders", "second toe" didn't.
  • Getting in and out is a step whenever it's where people get hurt: unracking and racking a bar, a bell upside down, lying face down with weights, a spin bike's flywheel, a band under tension.
  • Safety setup goes in step 1: a non-slip floor, space to land, a band with no cuts on an anchor it can't slide off.
  • A range landmark needs a form stop: "dumbbells at mid-shin, or higher if your back rounds". A fixed landmark alone tells a stiff beginner to round.
  • "How long?" is answered with a landmark, never a time: "until you are steady", "until your hands are beside your ears".
  • Balance work gets a way out: "Tap the other foot down if you lose balance."
  • Words that tripped a second-language reader: plant, stumble, freeze, slumped, drive, shrug, trunk, balls of your feet, hip bone, tears, "straight arms" (read as locked: say "elbows almost straight"). These go on a list every draft is checked against.
Never in steps · sets, reps, time, tempo, RPE, kilos, which side this athlete starts, home furniture, why the exercise is good, what to feel (that's a cue), another exercise's name or when to move to it (that's Regressions and Progressions).

Why: a handout alone gave more than twice the errors of video or live teaching (Reo & Mercer 2004), so where text is all there is, it has to be complete. Plain, explicit wording is understood far better than vague wording: 89% vs 53% in one study (Davis 2008). 65.5% of weight-room injuries seen in US emergency departments were weights dropping on the person (Kerr 2010), so free weights get a safe set-up and set-down.

Spine · for everyone

The 3 cues: finish the steps, never repeat them

Your words were: cues "complete the instruction, things to avoid, or important things to think about". With the steps carrying the setup, the three slots are free to do exactly that:

  1. ✓ Key point: the one thing to hold in mind during the set. The effect outside the body, or an analogy, works best: your own "Throw it through the wall, not at it", "Bounce off the floor like it's hot". About 10 words, no setup, no comma chains.
  2. ✓ Feel or check: for muscle, armour and mobility exercises, where you should feel it, in plain words ("Feel a stretch in the back of your standing thigh"). For jumps, sprints, throws, braking and heavy strength, a quick position check instead ("Chest and hips rise together as you stand"). The entry's first quality decides which, so two writers agree.
  3. ✕ Avoid: the one fault that most risks injury or ruins the rep, named in words, with the fix in the same line: "Knee falling inward. Keep it over your second toe." One fault, never two joined by "or", no reps or seconds. A stop rule is fine: "Low back stays on the floor. If it lifts, stop the set."

The pilot taught two things here. A line that only says the fix ("Knee stays over your toes") on the red line read to the test reader as "don't do this", so the fault has to be named. And the app tells the avoid line apart by red against green only, which fails for colour-blind athletes, so again the words must carry it. That is question Q2.

Why: focusing on the effect outside the body is a safe default with a small benefit (Chua 2021; the effect shrinks once you correct for publication bias, McKay 2024). A "feel the muscle" cue helps muscle work at light to moderate loads, not above about 60–80% of max (Calatayud 2016; Schoenfeld 2018), and on max-effort work it lowers force (Halperin 2016). A "don't do X" cue can pull you toward X under pressure or fatigue (Gorgulu 2019; Gray 2017), and the brain handles "not doing" by planning the alternative (Kühn & Brass 2010). Hence fix-first. People hold about four things at once (Cowan 2001), so three cues plus the steps is the ceiling.

Programme card · for this athlete · field "intent"

The pill: one change, from a fixed list

A pill is the only words on the closed card, so it should be the one thing this athlete acts on every rep that changes or adds to the Spine's normal way. Built from the pills you actually use:

At most two values, never two from the same group, kept in the one intent field joined by " · " as today, so no app change is needed. A new value joins the list only on your word, like a new quality. Old spellings map onto the list ("right leg first" → right side first, "fast turnover" → fast contact, "max power" → max intent). Whether braking drills and warm-up pace get their own group is Q4.

Never a pill · tempo or seconds ("3s eccentric" belongs in the tempo), a number or dose ("8 Rounds"), a muscle or category ("glute focus", "court transfer", which is on the public demo), a cue ("ribs down"), or the normal grip the steps already state.
Programme card · for this athlete · field "note"

The Coach's Note: only what's about them

One to three short sentences. The first sentence carries the point, because only about 75 characters show before "More ›". In order of value:

  1. Their starting weight, from their own logs and framed by the RPE (PRG-2): "Last cycle 20 kg for 10 at RPE 8. Try 22.5 kg and let the first set's RPE decide." With no logs, how to find it: "Start with a bell you can keep upright every rep." Unsupervised lifters pick weights that are too light (Mazzetti 2000), so this is the most useful thing a note can say.
  2. Their limit: a range, a pain stop, a side. A limit that changes a step says so: "Stop at the box, not the floor."
  3. Their kit or place: box height, a home anchor for a band, the court version.
  4. Their checked fallback (SEL-20), when the station is busy or it hurts: the swap that has been checked against their restrictions.
  5. An ask: "Film set 2 and send it." A fault seen on their own film belongs here too.
Never in a note · when to add weight (PRG-3: they hit the RPE and log, you change the card), general how-to (it's a step), a general cue, tempo, sets or reps, the grip (it's the pill), the reason when the exercise has a Because, an unchecked "machine busy" swap (the Spine's Alternatives cover that).
4 · The pilot

Twelve real exercises, written and tested

Eight with no video and four of your most-used. For each one a writer drafted the steps and cues from the Spine entry, your live cards and notes, and the course and testing apps. Then a test reader with no video read only the text: a 38-year-old in Tehran, four months in the gym, English as a second language. A senior coach checked it for accuracy and safety, and a third pass fixed what both found. Fresh readers then rated today's Spine text and the final text, each 1–5 for "I would do this right".

The readers and the coach were AI role-plays, not people. That makes this a fast, cheap screen that finds unclear words and missing steps; it doesn't prove real athletes will do better. The real check is a few of your athletes doing the first batch with no video, before the rest is written.

Glutes squeezing hard at the top
Arching your low back to go higher. Stop at the straight line.

Caught: the coach said ship it. Only the avoid line changed: the draft's "low back stays flat at the top" was read as "on the floor", which is impossible at the top. Today this exercise has 33 different cue sets across its 45 old cards.

What the pilot changed in the rule
  • Character count is the hard limit (under 90 per step). Setup steps that carry a safety detail run to about 16 words.
  • "One action per step" became "one phase per step": a Y-T raise lifts and lowers in one step, and a short fragment can give the end position ("Feet shoulder-width, toes out a little.").
  • Rack and bench lifts may need a 6th step (the squat's bail).
  • Write the steps first, then the cues from what's left, so a cue never repeats a step.
  • A cue is read on its own, so it points back to a landmark the steps gave ("Stop at the straight line"), never "flat" or "tight".
  • Twins aren't word swaps: Sprint to Two-Step Stop brakes earlier than the jog version and needs its own landmark.
  • Every line of a cue starts with a verb or "Feel".
5 · Your decisions

Thirteen questions, one line each to answer

The filled circle is my recommendation. Answer like Q1 B, Q2 A …, or say "all recommended" and name the ones you'd change. Nothing goes on the Spine, the app or a programme before you answer.

Q1 · CUES
What is the second cue?
  • Always where you feel it (CUE-1 as it is today).
  • A feel for muscle, armour and mobility exercises; a quick position check for power, speed, braking, rotation and heavy strength. The entry's first quality decides.
  • The writer chooses each time.

"Feel your calves" on a sprint says little, and on max-effort work an inward focus lowers force. B lets two writers agree.

Q2 · CUES
How is the avoid line written?
  • The fault, then the fix: "Knee falling inward. Keep it over your second toe."
  • The fix first: "Knee stays over your second toe, never falling inward." (the pilot's form)
  • As today: "Don't let the knee shoot inward."

Tested on all 12: fault-then-fix was understood on the first read 12 times out of 12. Fix-first read backwards on 8 ("avoid: knee stays over your toe"). Today's "Don't…" was clear on 8 and never says what to do instead. A also names the fault for colour-blind athletes, who can't tell the red line from the green ones, and ends on the right picture. The rewrite rides along with each entry's steps, not as a separate pass.

7 · Rollout

Five steps, each one shipped and checked

1

Rules and skills

The index lines, stories and history; /spine, /program-design, /program-engage, /program-assemble, /program-edit and /workout updated; the checker's warnings. No phone changes. From here, every new programme follows the four layers.

2

The server and coach.html

A steps column on the Spine, sent to phones by get_exercises() and saved by coach_save_spine(), tested on a throwaway database first. Every change is kept in spine_versions and backed up automatically. In coach.html: a "How to do it" box on each entry, showing Claude's draft until you save, and a pill box in the ✎ editor, which has none today.

3

The writing

The 12 pilot entries and the next 20 most-used no-video entries for you to read first. Then batches of about 40: the 71 no-video entries on live cards, then the rest by use. The app

Checked on a real phone width before it goes live.

Cleanup

Old pills mapped onto the list as each card is next saved (tempo words move into the tempo, "3s eccentric" ×40 included); the 32 athlete-specific old cues moved into notes; Library sessions' own cues dropped.
